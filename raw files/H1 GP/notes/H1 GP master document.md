# Insert

The insert contains 3 passages focusing on the same subject matter, and introduces concepts in each paragraph that need to be observed for answering questions. Each passage gets progressively shorter and less credible, with passage 3 literally being a half page opinion of a random dude on a blog.

Passage 1 the main passage, and presents the proposition argument for the topic at hand. The Short Answer Question (SAQ) focuses entirely on passage 1, usually spanning 6-9 questions.

Passage 2 is the rebuttal passage, presenting the opposition argument to passage 1. Passage 2 is always geared toward summary, being just long enough to make spamming every sentence impossible and also short enough such that you can clearly identify all relevant points

Passage 3 is the balancing passage, usually presenting an opinion more on the proposition side but provides key points of both sides of the argument. Passage 3 is very nuanced and neutral in its stand, giving factual, grounded anecdotes or generalisable opinions on the subject matter

# Question Paper

Question paper consists of 4 sections:

1. Short Answer Questions (SAQ)

   1. 6-9 questions of 1-3m each
   2. Follows a list of question types, each with specific answering format and techniques
   3. Carries bulk of your marks in P2
   4. Exclusive to passage 1
2. Summary

   1. Summarise passage 2 based on the prompt question given (usually not relevant as you will end up having to summarise every single point anyway)
   2. Total 14-17 possible points in passage 2, rule of thumb is to find what you think are 12 points in total. Only requires 10+ points to get all 8m
   3. Can go up to 10m (never been done before)
3. Not In Text (NIT)

   1. Draw comparisons across passage 3 to passage 1 and passage 3 to passage 2 (2 separate questions)
   2. Follows a very fixed and easy answering format
   3. New to H1 GP 8881 syllabus
   4. Have to use diff quote for both qn
   5. Can go up to 6m (3 qn)
4. Asshole Question (AQ)

   1. Whoever made this is an asshole
   2. Mini-essay
   3. Requires you to make you use points from the passages for thesis and quote them
   4. Intro, 2 body para (best to argue same side) and conclusion (eval centred)
   5. 1 good intro and 1 good body para with CU pushes to band 3 and awards 7/12m, which is the best strat for time constrained situations
   6. Full length essay with 1 strong body para, 1 normal/weak body para and 1 strong eval conclusion gives baseline 9/12m
   7. To go band 4 and get 10+ marks you highkey need to spend a good 25 minutes writing the AQ and need the entire AQ to be strong

# Marking scheme

GP marking doesnt follow a very fixed rubric, and gives benefit of doubt (BOD) for alot of cases — only in RI and A lvls. While the 8881 syllabus implicitly called for everything to be paraphrased in your own words as far as possible, the reality is that the only thing that needs to be paraphrased are the key scoring points. For parts of the sentence that are not essential for capturing the concept that scores the point, paraphrasing is completely optional. Of course, sometimes its just easier to paraphrase the whole entire thing in your own words, but doing that also risks accidentally being imprecise with your answer.

For AQ, the marking scheme is super arbitrary — kind of like the P1 marking rubric but just compressed into 4 bands and much more by vibes than by rubrics set in stone. As long as your intro looks good and is valid (3-5 lines), it automatically qualifies for pushing you into band 2-4. As long as you have 1 strong body para, it qualifies you for band 3, etc. for more details on the marking scheme and rubrics, can refer to the rubrics uploaded onto the drive

# Paraphrase

Most basic and important skill in GP P2. substituting a word/phrase for another with similar meaning using CMI:  
**C**ontext: does the same word fit into the passage?  
**M**eaning: does the word have similar connotations as the original?  
**I**ntensity: does it match the scale of the original word?

For common mistakes to avoid, use CATL: (“the missile knows where it is by knowing where it isn't" ahhh method)  
**C**ontext: context is missing  
**A**ccuracy: word is inaccurate in capturing idea  
**T**horoughness: incorrect intensity/no nuance  
**L**ift: lifted from passage

Words only need to be paraphrased if:

1. It is a key idea/point
2. It can be paraphrased while still following all the rules mentioned above

Words DO NOT need to be paraphrased if it cannot be paraphrased (using any other word just doesn't work; very rare case), or is not a keyword in the question.

## Logical suggestion

Logical Suggestion questions ask for a reason or explanation that is not explicitly stated in the text but is the only logical conclusion based on the context, using only **logic within the text** + common sense (not uncommon or rare sense)  
Keywords:

* "Suggest why..."
* "What does the author imply..."
* "Why is \[X] described as..." (when the text doesn't explicitly say "because...")
* "What does the phrase '\[X]' tell us about..."

Identification thought process (for the example qn):

1. Is there a direct answer? Does the text say "X happened because Y"?
2. If NO: The answer is hidden in the logic of the situation. You have to fill in the blank.
3. Is it a contradiction? (e.g., "sounds crazy"). If the text highlights a reaction, the "suggestion" is usually the reason for that reaction (e.g., it goes against the norm).

## Figurative language (Fig. lang.)

The author uses figurative language (simile, metaphor, etc.) to highlight a specific quality or characteristic. You must explain the effect of the language use and apply it to the context.

**THIS IS A SUBSET OF AUTHOR’S INTENTION: LANGUAGE USE**  
**DO NOT confuse it with the broader scope of the question type**

Steps:

1. Identify the figure of speech (e.g., "citadel", "blanket", "tsunami").
2. Describe the literal function of that image (e.g., a citadel protects against attacks).
3. Map it to the context (e.g., culture needs to be defended from change).
* Irony: Contrast between Expectation and Reality.
* Paradox: A contradiction that is nonetheless true.
* Analogy: compares two unlike things (X and Y) to highlight a specific quality or characteristic. Can be either metaphor (implicit comparison) or simile (explicit comparison, “X is as Y as Z”).

|Type|Identification|formula|
|-|-|-|
|Irony|"Unexpected outcome"|Expectation vs. Reality: "One would expect \[X], but in reality \[Y]."|
|Paradox|"True contradiction"|Seeming Contradiction vs. Truth: "It seems contradictory that \[X], yet it is true because \[Y]."|
|Analogy|"Image / Comparison"|Literal vs. Context: "Just as \[Image] \[function] to \[X], similarly \[Subject] \[function] to \[Y]."|

# **NIT summary**

So right this stuff is super easy:

The NIT section has only 2 questions, 1 undermine and 1 support question and these 2 questions are **standardised** across **EVERY SINGLE P2**.  
At its core, NIT questions test your cross-textual evaluation skills. You must find a specific point in Passage 3 that either corroborates (supports) or contradicts (undermines) a given claim from Passage 1 or 2.

Formula: \[identification of P3 idea] + \[justification — explain and compare]  
Steps:

1. Analyze passage 1/2 quote (mentally)
2. Identify passage 3 idea that supports/undermines
3. paraphrase idea
4. Justify and link passage 3 idea by comparing it to the passage 1/2 quote (paraphrased), similar to paired response questions

**The second mark is a because-noun, not a label.**  
Identification (right P3 stretch) is usually the easy mark. Justification must name the **concrete P3 noun** that maps onto the P1/P2 claim. “This supports because it shows sacrifice” / “this undermines because it is dangerous” is a vibe. It restates the *task*, not the idea.

If P3 has two candidate stretches, one is the ticket and one is a neighbour that would answer a **different** NIT:

|“That level of sacrifice is unacceptable to most”|People will cut plastic **only if they keep convenience**|“Don’t jump on the **bandwagon** / consider carefully” — that maps to *think before you act*, not to *sacrifice*|

# Intro to Summary

The summary question is a major component of the General Paper Paper 2, carrying  
8 marks for content (with significant weight on your overall 15-mark Language  
score).

\- Summary focuses exclusively on Passage 2.

* Passage 2 is intentionally written to be "summary-friendly"—meaning it is  
just long enough to make copying every sentence impossible within the word  
limit, but dense enough that identifying the relevant points is  
straightforward.
* You are required to extract relevant points based on a specific prompt,  
paraphrase them in your own words, and present them in continuous prose  
within a strict 120-word limit.
* There are usually between 11 to 15 possible points hidden in the text. You  
only need to successfully identify and paraphrase 10 points to secure the  
maximum 8 marks.

# Summary Technique (Step-by-Step)

Step 1: Analyze the Prompt Identify the exact parameters of the question.  
Summary prompts often have two parts (e.g., "Summarise why people do not slow  
down to reflect AND why they should do so"). Missing one half of the prompt will  
severely cap your maximum score.

Step 2: Identify and Bracket (Isolating Points) Comb through Passage 2 and  
bracket the points that directly answer the prompt.

\- Ignore elaborate anecdotes, statistics, and repetitive elaborations.

* Rule of thumb: Aim to identify 12 points. This gives you a 2-point safety  
net in case you misinterpret a point or fail to paraphrase one adequately.

**One paragraph, three jobs — do not give them three numbers.**

1. **Announce** — a topic sentence (“offer help and solace”) often *names* two units; it is not itself a third point.
2. **Cash** — “not only raise funds but also bolstered morale” *is* help and solace. Those two clauses are two marks.
3. **Restate** — Glastonbury “channel millions” / “support the world’s most vulnerable” is usually still the funds point, not a new one. A closing “heritage food does not stand a chance” is a conclusion, not a reason. **Do not hunt it as a fifth point.**

Swap test: if you can swap the two nouns and both still read as the same benefit, they were merged. “Raise funds” and “bolstered morale” cannot swap. “Seek new food adventures” and “queue at Michelin” cannot swap.

If one sentence still contains **two paraphrasable payloads**, a marker may pay both. Hunting a ghost point after that does not add a mark; it costs words and time.

Step 3: Draft and Paraphrase Translate your bracketed points into your own  
words. Use the APT / CMI rules for paraphrasing:

\- Accuracy / Context: Ensure the new word fits the original context of the  
sentence.

* Precision / Meaning: Capture the exact nuance without being vague.
* Thoroughness / Intensity: Completeness of the idea. Ensure you capture  
cause-and-effect relationships or intensity modifiers (e.g., "significantly  
contributing" must be paraphrased to capture both the action and the scale).

Step 4: Connect and Count String your paraphrased points together into a single,  
cohesive paragraph.

\- Use simple, logical connectors (e.g., Furthermore, Conversely, Consequently,  
Ultimately).

* Write in continuous prose. Bullet points will be penalised.
* Count your words strictly. Examiners will draw a line at exactly the 120th  
word, and any points made after that line will yield zero marks.

# Tips and Tricks

What NOT to Paraphrase:

\- The Main Topic/Subject: You do not need to endlessly find synonyms for the  
core topic of the summary (e.g., "heritage food", "music", "climate change",  
"meritocracy").

* Proper Nouns \& Universal Terms: Words like "Earth", "society", "government",  
"humanity", or specific names do not need to be forced into awkward  
synonyms.
* Examples: Exclude them entirely. Exception: If an example is the only way  
the author conveys a point, extract the underlying general idea from it  
(e.g., instead of "McDonalds and KFC", write "fast food establishments").

Word Count Hacks:

\- Hyphenated words count as ONE word. (e.g., "well-being", "self-reflection",  
"fast-paced").

* Cut the fluff. Do not write long introductory sentences like, "The author  
states that there are many benefits to owning a pet, the first of which  
is..." Start immediately: "Owning a pet increases physical activity..."
* Keep connectors short. Use "Also," or "Next," instead of "Another point to  
consider is that...".

Matching the Subject: Ensure your paraphrased points align with the grammatical  
subject of the prompt. If the prompt asks for "the benefits of music," your  
points should be framed around what music does (e.g., "Music alleviates fear,"  
"Music boosts group identity").

# Step-by-Step Mini-Essay Structure

## 1\. Introduction (3–5 lines)

Keep it extremely short. Your intro automatically qualifies you for Bands 2–4 as long as it is valid and clearly establishes the context and balance.

* Link to Singapore: Briefly hint at your society (e.g., hyper-modern, pragmatic, meritocratic). Do not use examples here.
* State Balance (Thesis): Clearly outline the opposing views and your overarching stand with brief reasons.
* Template: "While the view that \[Author's View 1] has merit in Singapore due to \[SG Reason 1], undeniably, \[Author's View 2] is far more applicable because \[SG Reason 2]."

## 2\. Body Paragraphs 1 \& 2 (The Core)

Argue the same side for both paragraphs if possible (easier to sustain a coherent thesis). Every paragraph must contain Balance, Apt Illustration, and Convincing Evaluation.

1. Claim: Quote or paraphrase a manageable, opinion-based claim from the passage. (Ensure the idea is reflected in your Stand, Opposing View, and Rebuttal).
2. Explanation (EX): Briefly explain what the claim means if it requires clarification (PARAPHRASE).
3. Stand (SV) + Reason: State your view on its applicability to Singapore (not whether you agree/disagree with the author generally). Provide a reason rooted in a specific SG Feature (e.g., Kiasu culture, government pragmatism).
4. Illustration + Insight: Provide 2 Examples (1 personal example generalisable to society + 1 national-level example). Combine them to form an Insight (a key message, trend, or observation arising from the examples). This proves strong Contextual Understanding (CU).
5. Opposing View (OPV) / Limitation: Acknowledge the other side. Empathize with the limitation of your stand (Concession).
6. Rebuttal \& Evaluation (EV): Refute the OPV to push your paragraph into Band 3/4. Use one of these evaluation strategies:

   1. Deeper SG Features: Explain why the OPV fails by bringing in SG's historical developments, geographical constraints, or political culture.
   2. EV Lens: Contrast Ideal vs. Reality, Short-run vs. Long-run, or Majority vs. Minority.
   3. Exception / SALT: Show why the OPV only applies to a very niche exception.
7. Concluding Sentence: Link back to the Supported View (SV) and the question prompt.

## 3\. Conclusion (1–2 lines)

You are not heavily assessed on the conclusion, but omitting it kills the vibes.

\- Provide at the very least, a one-liner restating your thesis.

* Bonus for Band 4: End with a reflective evaluation or call to action (a  
hopeful outlook for Singapore's future regarding the issue).

# my tip(s)

1\. Evaluation (EV) ≠ Illustration (EX)

\- The Trap: Students confuse giving examples (Illustration) with Evaluation.  
Merely listing SG policies (e.g., "Singapore has the Green Plan 2030") is  
just descriptive.

* The Fix: Evaluation is the ability to judge why the claim applies based on  
the logic of Singapore's context. You must explain the underlying history,  
political culture, or social norms that cause Singaporeans to act a certain  
way, using the example only as support for your logic.

2\. Engage with Ideas in FULL (=Do Not Chunk)

\- The Trap: If an author claims "A causes B", students only evaluate "A" or  
"B" independently.

* The Fix: You must evaluate the relationship. For example, if the claim is  
"personal action drives political action," you must evaluate the link  
between the two in Singapore, not just prove that Singaporeans take personal  
action.

3\. Choose Your Claims Wisely

\- Pick Opinions, Not Facts: Fact-based observations (e.g., "everyone faces  
choices that carry a climate cost") hold true everywhere and offer zero  
evaluative potential. Pick controversial, opinion-based claims.

* Don't Pick What You Can't Handle: Do not pick a claim that is too long (you  
will fail to address all parts of it) or a claim you only partially  
understand.

4\. Beware the Straw Man Fallacy in Balance

\- When writing your Opposing View (OPV), do not reduce the opposing viewpoint  
to something silly just so it's easy to rebut. Show genuine empathy and  
acceptance of the viewpoint's merit in Singapore, and then make it clear why  
your SV still overrides it.

5\. Avoid Chaining Assertions

\- Listing three separate, unrelated reasons why a claim applies to Singapore  
does not equal "depth." Deep evaluation means taking one strong reason and  
fleshing out the logical links thoroughly.

6\. Promise big, deliver big

\- “i largely agree… because (big boy thesis)”

7\. Laser focus the subject matter

\- if the question asks for “value of X is declining…” the debate should be about value of X declining vs value of X NOT declining.
