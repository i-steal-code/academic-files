# Pillar: Technology, work and the arts

**Engine:** A technology's effect on people depends less on what it can do than on who sets the terms of its use. Organised workers can bargain over it (the Hollywood strikes); a state can punish its abuse after the harm appears (Korea's deepfake law); a company that owns the infrastructure can decide who uses it (Starshield in Ukraine); and a small state can ration the scarce resource the technology needs (Singapore's power for data centres). The arts sit inside the same question: they matter where people insist that human authorship deserves recognition, and where a society argues over what its artists may say.

**Use for:** control of technology, AI and fear, work and automation, artists' livelihoods, the value of the arts, gender and technology, crime and punishment online, infrastructure and power, Singapore's digital economy.

**Anchors:** A8 (the Hollywood strikes) and A9 (Korea's deepfake crisis) in `02`. The finance of AI is in `04` (A5); the reading decline and social media are in `08` (A13).

## A. AI and work: who sets the terms

**The Hollywood strikes (A8).** In 2023 the Writers Guild of America struck for 148 days and the actors' union SAG-AFTRA for 118. The writers' settlement stops AI-generated text from counting as "literary material", so it cannot reduce a writer's credit or pay, and requires studios to disclose when material given to writers was produced by AI. The actors won rules requiring specific consent and payment whenever a performer's digital replica is used. Neither union won a ban on training AI with their work; that question went to the courts. The protections cover unionised film and television workers in the US, not the freelance illustrators, translators and musicians who sell their work alone.

**Which office jobs an algorithm takes over.** Within administrative work, an algorithm takes over a task when three things are true: the material is already text or numbers in a system, the rule for handling it is stable, and a correct result can be checked without anyone in the room.

- **Most exposed:** data-entry clerks, typists, routine bookkeeping and payroll clerks, and call-centre staff working from a script. The International Labour Organization's exposure index places data-entry clerks, typists and accounting clerks in its highest band, and the World Economic Forum's 2025 list of fastest-declining roles names data-entry clerks, bank tellers, cashiers and postal clerks, because an app or script already completes the standard transaction.
- **Exposed on paperwork, kept for exceptions:** secretaries, schedulers and executive assistants lose first drafts and filing, but someone still decides which meeting the principal takes and which caller to refuse. Medical and legal secretaries are less exposed, because an error in a patient file or court bundle creates liability.
- **Least exposed to software:** mail sorters, couriers and inventory clerks, because the record can be digital but the object still has to be carried.

**The mechanism.** Where the job is a rule applied to a record, the algorithm does the task and the post shrinks, as has already happened to routine bank telling. Where the job is a person deciding an exception, or carrying the thing the record refers to, the software changes the task and leaves the post. One 2025 study of US workers found that those combining high exposure with the least savings and least portable skills, about 6.1 million people, are concentrated in clerical and administrative support, which is why displacement strands this group first.

## B. Deepfakes and the limits of control (A9)

In August 2024 South Korean media exposed Telegram chat rooms in which users, many of them teenagers, made and shared sexual deepfakes of women and girls, including classmates and teachers. Police handled more than 800 deepfake sex-crime cases in 2024 by late September. A law in force from 16 October 2024 made possessing, buying, saving or watching such material punishable by up to three years in prison, and raised the maximum sentence for making or distributing it to seven years.

**What it shows about control.** The state acted within weeks, so technology can be controlled after harm becomes visible. Prevention is harder: the tools are free, the images spread before any law can act, and the platform's servers lie outside Korea, so enforcement depends on a foreign company's cooperation. Control of technology increasingly means control of the platforms that distribute it.

**What it shows about gender.** A technology marketed as neutral widens an existing inequality when those already inclined to exploit women can use it at no cost. The same platforms also let women organise and expose abuse, so the effect on equality depends on who uses the tool and whether the law follows.

## C. Infrastructure: who owns the pipes

**Starshield in Ukraine (`03` §G).** In a war fought largely with drones, one American company's satellite network decides whether Ukraine's drones can fly. In October 2026 the military network was throttled for more than a day while US officials pressed Kyiv to stop striking Russian refineries; two US officials told Reuters the order came from the White House, which denies it. A private firm's infrastructure had become an instrument of state pressure, which shows corporate and state power working together rather than one replacing the other.

**Singapore's data centres.** Singapore hosts so many servers because the undersea internet cables of the region come ashore there, banks and governments trust it with other people's data, and the Economic Development Board recruited data centres as the next version of the port: a resource-poor island stays rich by being the node everyone must pass through. The government describes more than 1.6 gigawatts of data-centre capacity as the base of the digital economy. The constraint is electricity: in 2024 data centres used about 6.5 terawatt-hours, slightly more than a tenth of the electricity Singapore consumed, and AI chips draw far more power than older servers. After pausing new data centres from 2019 to 2022, the state now rations them. In August 2026 it provisionally allocated 200 megawatts among Digital Realty, Equinix, Keppel and ST Telemedia from more than twenty bids, on condition that the winners draw at least half their power from lower-carbon sources, use liquid cooling, and bring research and advanced computing rather than only rented floor space.

**What the two cases show together.** Infrastructure gives its owner leverage over everyone who depends on it, whether that owner is a company (Starshield) or a state (Singapore's grid). A small state without natural resources can still set terms for the world's largest technology firms when it controls something they need, here a connected, trusted location with limited power.

## D. The arts: what they are worth

**Recognition against official approval.** In May 2015 the National Arts Council withdrew an S$8,000 publishing grant from Sonny Liew's graphic novel *The Art of Charlie Chan Hock Chye*, saying its retelling of Singapore's history "potentially undermines the authority or legitimacy of the government and its public institutions". The book sold out its first print run, won the Singapore Literature Prize in 2016, and in 2017 made Liew the first Singaporean to win Eisner Awards (three of them, the comics industry's highest honours). The National Arts Council later congratulated him and noted that he had received other state support. The gap between official disapproval and recognition at home and abroad is the analysis: the state funds the arts as shared identity, but draws a line where a work contests the official account of its own history.

**Ranking under strain.** Live performance was the first sector to close in the pandemic and the last to reopen; calling it "non-essential" was a budgeting category, not a judgement of its value to people (`07` §D). In the SG60 year, a S$100 SG Culture Pass for adult citizens nudged households towards museums and performances, a nudge rather than a change in how families under pressure rank a ticket against tuition (`09` §D).

**Human authorship.** The Hollywood strikes (A8) show audiences and workers acting as if human authorship matters, even when a machine can produce the text.

**Value endings** (`01` §6.5). For "nothing more than a luxury" titles, the OV shows how a pragmatic society ranks the arts under strain; the SVs show the goods that remain: shared experience, civic speech, memory and recognition.

## G. Exam mistakes

- Writing that AI will replace all jobs. Name the tasks (a stable rule applied to a record) and the groups (clerical workers with few savings) most exposed.
- Treating the Hollywood settlement as a ban on AI. It set rules on credit, consent and pay; training data went to the courts.
- Writing that Korea "solved" deepfakes. It punished them faster; prevention still depends on foreign platforms.
- Stating as fact that the White House ordered the Starshield slowdown. US officials told Reuters it did; the White House denies it.
- Using *Charlie Chan Hock Chye* only as proof that Singapore has art. Its value is the gap between official disapproval and recognition.
