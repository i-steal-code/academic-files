# Anchor bank: sixteen examples, many questions

This is the whole memorisation load. Each anchor is one example, or a tight pair, that can answer questions in several categories once its topic sentence is re-aimed at the new keyword (argument adaptation, `01` §5.2). The pillars (`03`–`09`) hold the depth behind each anchor; open one when a paragraph needs a second fact.

**How to memorise an anchor.** Learn the hook, the three facts, and the two lines on what it proves and where it stops. Test yourself in that order: from the hook alone, recall the facts; from the facts, say the mechanism; from the mechanism, say the condition under which it fails. The topic sentences under "Aim it at" show the method and need not be learned by heart.

**How the anchors are numbered.** They follow the pillars in order, so the anchors that share a pillar sit together.

## Politics and power (`03`)

### A1. The 2024 US election
**Hook:** voters judge governments by their own bills.

1. November 2024: Donald Trump won the presidency, the popular vote and both houses of Congress, six months after a New York jury convicted him on 34 counts of falsifying business records.
2. Voters named the prices of food, rent and energy as their main concern.
3. October 2026: with diesel expensive before the midterms, he struck a deal to buy Russian diesel, reversing years of US pressure on Moscow.

**Proves.** When living costs rise faster than wages, most voters judge a government by what they pay each month, and arguments about institutions, character or foreign commitments weigh less than prices.
**Stops.** It describes the median voter in a polarised two-party democracy. It says little about activists, compulsory-voting systems, or Singapore, where the May 2025 election (PAP 65.6%) rewarded a record of delivery.

Aim it at:
- *‘Governments today need to work harder to gain the support of their people.’* (2023 VJC). Governments must work harder for support when rising living costs make voters judge them by household bills, since a record of competence earned in calmer years no longer protects an incumbent whose citizens cannot afford rent.
- *‘A popular leader is a good leader.’* (2025 TMJC). Popularity measures how well a leader answers voters' immediate grievances, which is not the same as governing well, because policies that relieve prices before an election can undermine commitments that protect citizens later.
- *Should what leaders do in their private lives matter to the public?* (2026 MI). Private conduct should matter when it bears on the honesty with which public power is used, yet voters set it aside readily when they believe a candidate will lower what they pay.

### A2. The Gaza Board of Peace
**Hook:** a mandate without enforcement.

1. A ceasefire under Trump's 20-point plan began on 10 October 2025. UN Security Council Resolution 2803 (17 November 2025, 13–0, China and Russia abstaining) endorsed a Board of Peace chaired by Trump and authorised a stabilisation force.
2. A year later no force has deployed and the Palestinian committee appointed to run Gaza has not entered it, because Israel and Hamas each insist that the other move first.
3. The Board's recovery plan is worth $2.45 billion; the EU and UN estimate reconstruction needs at $71.4 billion.

**Proves.** An international organisation can confer legitimacy quickly, but it cannot enforce a plan whose armed parties each wait for the other to concede, so authority on paper outruns capacity on the ground.
**Stops.** The ceasefire has largely held, and the Security Council did act after failing to for most of the war. The case shows the limits of enforcement, not the irrelevance of the institution.

Aim it at:
- *‘A lot of talk and very little action.’ To what extent is this true of international organisations?* (2025 EJC). International organisations produce more agreement than action when they depend on member states for troops and money, since a resolution can authorise a force that no state will send into a contested territory.
- *How far do international organisations remain crucial amid global instability?* (2026 JPJC). They remain crucial because they turn a great power's plan into an arrangement other states can join, even when they lack the means to enforce what they endorse.
- *Should bigger states involve themselves in the affairs of other countries?* (2023 SAJC). Bigger states are justified in intervening when they have the means and the will to sustain a settlement, since a truce brokered without a force to secure it leaves civilians waiting for reconstruction that does not arrive.

### A3. Myanmar and ASEAN
**Hook:** an election without a contest, and a regional body that can refuse recognition but cannot enforce its own plan.

1. February 2021: the military seized power. In April 2021 ASEAN agreed a Five-Point Consensus (end the violence, hold dialogue among all parties, deliver aid, appoint an envoy), which the junta has largely ignored; ASEAN bars its leaders from summits.
2. 28 December 2025 to 25 January 2026: a three-phase election run by the junta, with Aung San Suu Kyi's party dissolved. The military-backed USDP won 232 of 263 contested lower-house seats, and the military keeps a quarter of all seats regardless.
3. ASEAN sent no observers and does not recognise the result, though Thailand called it "the start of a transition". Singapore's foreign minister, Vivian Balakrishnan, said legitimacy requires an end to the fighting and dialogue with all parties. Singapore chairs ASEAN in 2027.

**Proves.** Voting alone does not make a democracy, because the contest depends on conditions that popularity cannot supply: parties allowed to run and voters safe to choose. And a body that works by consensus and non-interference can withhold legitimacy but cannot enforce its own plan.
**Stops.** ASEAN's refusal still denies the junta the legitimacy it held the election to win, and members disagree, so the body is divided rather than idle. Myanmar is an extreme; use it as a limit, not as a typical democracy.

Aim it at:
- *‘Democracy today is nothing more than a popularity contest.’* Democracy is more than a popularity contest because the contest itself depends on freedoms that popularity cannot supply, as Myanmar's 2025–26 election showed when a vote held without the main opposition party produced a result its neighbours refused to recognise.
- *‘A lot of talk and very little action.’* (2025 EJC). Regional organisations talk more than they act when their own rules forbid interference in members' affairs, since ASEAN can withhold recognition from Myanmar's junta but has not enforced the plan it agreed in 2021.
- *Should bigger states involve themselves in the affairs of other countries?* (2023 SAJC), as one comparison sentence. Non-interference protects small states from larger ones, but Myanmar shows its cost: neighbours who will not intervene can only watch a junta ignore the plan they agreed.

## Economy, corporations and the race (`04`)

### A4. The Iran war and the Strait of Hormuz
**Hook:** a war decided by fuel prices.

1. 28 February 2026: the US and Israel began large-scale strikes on Iran, and Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, which normally carries about a fifth of the world's oil.
2. Brent crude rose from about $72 to nearly $120 a barrel. US inflation reached 3.7% by September, and the Federal Reserve raised interest rates.
3. Singapore, which imports all its energy, felt the shock in electricity, transport and food prices.

**Proves.** In an interdependent economy a conflict among a few states transmits its costs to every state that trades through the same chokepoint, so countries with no say in the decision pay part of its price.
**Stops.** Interdependence also restrains: the oil shock raised pressure on the US to seek a truce. Exposure is not helplessness, and Singapore's reserves and diversified suppliers softened the blow.

Aim it at:
- *Is it right for a leader to place national interests above global responsibilities?* (2025 RI). A leader who places national interests above global stability misjudges both when his country depends on the same trade routes as everyone else, since a war that closes a strait raises the prices his own voters pay.
- *‘Being a small country is a good thing as much as a bad thing.’* (2023 TMJC). Smallness is a liability when decisions taken by larger powers raise a small state's import bills overnight, though a small state that hedges its suppliers and holds reserves can absorb a shock it could not prevent.
- *Assess the claim that in our globalised world food security would not be an issue today.* (2022 CJC). Globalisation makes food security more fragile when supply depends on routes that a single conflict can close, because cheaper imports in normal years become dearer imports in every crisis.

### A5. TSMC and CoreWeave
**Hook:** a useful technology can be overfinanced.

1. October 2026: chip shares fell for eight sessions in a row. CoreWeave, which owes about $50 billion and still loses money, was priced by markets as more likely than not to default within five years.
2. The same week, TSMC reported a sixth consecutive quarter of record profit and forecast fourth-quarter revenue 58% higher than a year earlier.
3. Why the firms keep building: Sundar Pichai (2024) said the risk of underinvesting was "dramatically greater" than the risk of overinvesting; Mark Zuckerberg (2025) accepted the risk of "misspending a couple of hundred billion dollars".

**Proves.** Whether a technology works and whether the firms that borrowed to build it survive are separate questions. Each firm keeps spending because falling behind looks costlier than wasting money, and it fails if revenue arrives more slowly than its debts fall due.
**Stops.** Markets can be wrong, and a default-risk price is not a forecast. The case shows financial fragility, not a verdict on AI's usefulness.

Aim it at:
- *Consider the view that science and technology promise more than they deliver.* (2025 TJC). AI delivers real demand, as TSMC's record orders show, but the promises made to lenders outrun what the technology can earn in time, so the disappointment falls on financiers rather than users.
- *Evaluate the view that modern technology can no longer be effectively controlled.* (2026 EJC). Technology remains subject to control where it depends on borrowed money, because rising interest rates can halt an expansion that no regulator chose to slow.
- *How far is economic success an accurate reflection of a country's overall well-being?* (2025 SAJC), as one sentence for Singapore. About three-quarters of Singapore's 5.4% growth in the third quarter of 2026 came from technology-related industries while households paid more for imported energy and food, so aggregate growth rose while living costs squeezed ordinary families.

### A6. China's involution: the race no one can leave
**Hook:** every player can see the race is wasteful, and none can afford to stop first.

1. April 2021: Luo Huazhong's post "Lying flat is justice" described living on about 200 yuan a month outside the race for overtime, a flat, marriage and a child. State newspapers called lying flat shameful and censors deleted the post. In August 2021 China's top court and labour ministry declared the "996" schedule (9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week) illegal.
2. May 2025: BYD cut its cheapest car to 55,800 yuan, and Great Wall's chairman warned that the car industry had an "Evergrande" waiting to happen. In June, 17 carmakers pledged to pay suppliers within 60 days.
3. July 2025: Xi Jinping ordered action against "disorderly low-price competition", after factory-gate prices had fallen for 33 months in a row.

**Proves.** When the reward stays fixed while effort or spending rises, each player keeps racing because stopping alone means losing. Young workers withdraw quietly, firms push costs onto suppliers, and the state ends up attacking the response (lying flat, price cuts) rather than the rules that reward the race.
**Stops.** The same competition gave China the world's cheapest electric cars, so involution wastes money but also spreads technology. Most young people who "lie flat" still work, and there is no headcount of them.

Aim it at:
- *Examine the claim that the world is too competitive today.* (2023 ACJC). Competition becomes excessive when the effort needed to win keeps rising while the prize stays fixed, as both China's carmakers and its young office workers found in the same decade.
- *‘Hard work is the key to success.’ Discuss in relation to your society.* (2023 ASRJC). Hard work remains the key to success only where its rewards keep pace with its costs; young Chinese workers who concluded that overtime no longer bought a home stopped trying harder, and Singapore's fertility rate of 0.87 suggests the same private calculation made without a slogan.
- *Can the government ever balance national interest and individual freedom?* (2025 NJC). A government fails to strike that balance when it treats a private decision to work less as a threat to the nation, as Beijing did by deleting the lying-flat post and, in 2026, blaming the idea on hostile foreign forces.

## Environment and cooperation (`05`)

### A7. Montreal and Paris
**Hook:** why one environmental treaty worked and the other struggles.

1. The Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out nearly 99% of controlled ozone-depleting substances. Substitutes existed, few firms made the chemicals, and a Multilateral Fund paid developing countries' costs. The ozone layer is projected to recover to 1980 levels around 2040 (around 2066 over Antarctica).
2. The Paris Agreement (2015) lets each country set its own voluntary target, with no penalty for missing it.
3. The US left Paris in 2020, rejoined in 2021, left again with effect from January 2026, and has given notice that it will leave the UN climate convention in February 2027.

**Proves.** Environmental agreements succeed when compliance is cheap, polluters are few and someone pays poorer countries to comply. Climate change has none of these conditions, because fossil fuels run every economy and the largest historical emitter can leave whenever its domestic politics change.
**Stops.** Paris has not failed outright: clean-energy costs have fallen sharply and many countries keep cutting emissions without the US. The contrast explains slow progress, not impossibility.
**Same pattern outside climate (one sentence, `04` §H).** In 2021 more than 130 countries agreed a 15% global minimum tax to end the race to cut corporate tax, and in June 2025 the G7 exempted American-owned firms from key parts of it, so a rule ends a race only while the largest player stays bound.

Aim it at:
- *‘So close yet so far.’ Is this the outcome of our efforts to address climate change?* (2023 DHS). Climate efforts are close in technology but far in politics, because the tools to cut emissions are increasingly affordable while the agreement meant to coordinate their use depends on pledges any government can abandon.
- *How far do you agree that environmental protection is more of a political problem than a scientific one?* (2026 NJC). Environmental protection becomes a political problem once science has supplied workable alternatives, as the ozone layer's recovery shows, since the obstacle to climate action is now governments' unwillingness to impose costs on their own voters.
- *Consider the argument that cooperation among countries is only motivated by self-interest.* (2025 DHS). Cooperation can go beyond self-interest when richer states pay poorer ones to comply, as the Multilateral Fund did, though such generosity is easiest when the cost is small.

## Technology, work and the arts (`06`)

### A8. The 2023 Hollywood strikes
**Hook:** workers, not regulators, set the first rules for AI in film.

1. The Writers Guild of America struck for 148 days. Its settlement stops AI-generated text from counting as "literary material", so it cannot reduce writers' credit or pay, and requires studios to disclose AI-produced material.
2. The actors' union SAG-AFTRA struck for 118 days and won consent and payment whenever a performer's digital replica is used.
3. Neither union won a ban on training AI with their work; that question was left to the courts.

**Proves.** A new technology's effect on workers depends less on what it can do than on who has the power to set the terms of its use; organised workers turned a threat to their livelihoods into rules on consent and pay.
**Stops.** The protections cover unionised US film and television workers. Freelance illustrators, translators and musicians, including most artists in Singapore, have no comparable bargaining power.

Aim it at:
- *Assess whether the arts continue to matter in societies driven by science and technology.* (2026 CJC). The arts continue to matter in a technology-driven society when people insist that human authorship deserves recognition, as Hollywood's writers and actors showed by striking for months to protect their credit and their likeness.
- *Assess the view that it is difficult for artists today to earn a living from their work.* (2026 EJC). Earning a living from art is hardest for artists who sell their work alone, since organised writers and actors secured rules on AI that freelancers cannot negotiate for themselves.
- *Evaluate the view that modern technology can no longer be effectively controlled.* (2026 EJC). Technology can still be controlled where it is used, because contracts that require consent and payment for digital replicas govern AI without banning it.

### A9. South Korea's deepfake crisis
**Hook:** cheap tools let anyone attack a woman's dignity at scale.

1. August 2024: Korean media exposed Telegram chat rooms in which users, many of them teenagers, made and shared sexual deepfakes of women and girls, including classmates and teachers.
2. Police handled more than 800 deepfake sex-crime cases in 2024 by late September.
3. A law in force from 16 October 2024 made possessing, buying, saving or watching such material punishable by up to three years in prison, and raised the maximum for making or distributing it to seven years.

**Proves.** A technology marketed as neutral widens existing inequalities when it is used by those already inclined to exploit them, because generative tools make abuse cheap, fast and easy to spread.
**Stops.** The state responded within weeks, so the harm can be punished even if it cannot be prevented; but the platform's servers lie outside Korea, so enforcement depends on a foreign company's cooperation.

Aim it at:
- *How far has modern technology impacted the pursuit of gender equality?* (2024 MI). Technology sets back gender equality when tools that cost nothing let men degrade women at scale, as Korea's deepfake crisis showed, even as the same platforms help women organise and expose abuse.
- *Evaluate the view that modern technology can no longer be effectively controlled.* (2026 EJC). Technology can still be controlled after it causes visible harm, as Korea's law punishing even the viewing of sexual deepfakes shows, but cheap tools spread faster than laws are written, so control usually arrives after the victims.
- *To what extent is society justified in fearing developments in artificial intelligence?* Fear of AI is most justified where it lets individuals inflict personal, hard-to-reverse harm, since a deepfake can be made in minutes and shared before any law can intervene.

## COVID, health and trust (`07`)

### A10. Circuit Breaker and the dormitories
**Hook:** the same rule, unequal risk.

1. April 2020: Singapore's Circuit Breaker closed most workplaces and schools.
2. In the same months, infection spread fastest through foreign-worker dormitories, where men slept twelve or more to a room, while professionals worked safely from home.
3. The state responded with mass testing, state-funded treatment and new dormitory standards.

**Proves.** A crisis exposes inequalities that already existed: identical rules produced very different risks because risk followed housing, and housing followed class and job.
**Stops.** The state corrected the conditions once they were seen. The case shows what a society had accepted without looking, not that it refused to act.

Aim it at:
- *‘Invisible people living invisible lives.’ How true is this of the world today?* (2023 RVHS). Low-wage migrant workers live invisible lives when an economy houses them apart from the citizens they serve, and they become visible only when a crisis turns their living conditions into a public health risk.
- *‘The greatest obstacle to ending social inequality is human greed.’* (2022 NJC). Inequality persists less because of individual greed than because economies are designed to keep low-wage labour cheap, which the pandemic exposed when infection followed housing rather than morality.
- *Examine the view that in all areas of life, flexibility is key.* (2025 CJC). Flexibility proves decisive when conditions change faster than rules can anticipate, as Singapore's shift from Circuit Breaker to "living with COVID" showed, though only a state able to enforce strict rules can relax them in an orderly way.

### A11. mRNA vaccines and COVAX
**Hook:** science outran trust and fairness.

1. BioNTech/Pfizer and Moderna produced mRNA vaccines in under a year, building on decades of earlier research and emergency funding.
2. Rich countries pre-ordered doses for their own populations, and COVAX, the scheme to share vaccines with poorer countries, fell short of its targets.
3. Many well-educated people still refused vaccination.

**Proves.** Scientific capability can be produced faster than the political will to distribute it or the public trust to use it, so whether a breakthrough improves the world depends on who receives it and who believes it.
**Stops.** The vaccines saved millions of lives where they were used. The failures were in distribution and trust, which are political and social problems, not scientific ones.

Aim it at:
- *‘Despite all the scientific advancements that we have made, we have not created a better world for mankind.’* (2022 NYJC). Scientific advances have made the world better for those who receive them, but the speed at which rich countries secured mRNA vaccines for themselves shows that science's benefits are shared only as fairly as politics allows.
- *Consider the argument that cooperation among countries is only motivated by self-interest.* (2025 DHS). Cooperation gives way to self-interest when supplies are scarce and leaders answer to domestic voters, as declared solidarity yielded to national pre-orders in the vaccine race.
- *‘It is too easy to stir up fear today.’* (2023 RVHS). Fear spreads easily when officials speak with more certainty than the evidence supports and later reverse themselves, because each reversal gives those who sell doubt a reason to be believed.

## Education and meritocracy (`08`)

### A12. Reforms that leave the exam untouched
**Hook:** families compete for whatever the gate rewards.

1. Japan's Yutori reform (2002) cut about 30% of curriculum content, then was reversed by about 2011 after PISA scores fell and families turned to cram schools.
2. China's Double Reduction (2021) banned for-profit academic tutoring. Tutoring moved underground and became dearer, poorer students lost cheap group classes, and the gaokao stayed unchanged.
3. South Korea removed "killer questions" from its national exam in 2023, yet private education spending reached a record 29.2 trillion won in 2024.

**Proves.** A reform that changes what happens in school but leaves the exam that decides futures in place does not reduce pressure; it moves the pressure to where wealthy families can pay for it.
**Stops.** Double Reduction did improve sleep and well-being for many children in the short term. The lesson is about reform without changing selection, not that reform is pointless.

Aim it at:
- *‘Strong ambition, weak execution.’ Is this a fair description of governance today?* (2025 HCI). Governments execute ambitious reforms poorly when they regulate behaviour without changing the incentive behind it, as China found when banning tutoring left the gaokao, and so the demand for tutoring, intact.
- *‘Children grow up too fast today.’ How true is this of your society?* (2023 NJC). Children in exam-driven societies grow up too fast when the selection points that decide their futures arrive at twelve, because families then treat childhood as preparation and fill it with tuition.
- *To what extent should the beauty industry be regulated?* (2022 TJC), as one comparison sentence. Regulation protects consumers only when it also addresses the demand behind a product, since bans on something people still want push its sale into channels that are harder to supervise.

### A13. The US reading decline
**Hook:** when the feed replaces the classroom.

1. US 13-year-olds' reading scores in 2025 were not significantly different from 1971, and the decline began around 2013, before COVID.
2. The share of 13-year-olds who read for fun almost daily fell from 27% (2012) to 14% (2023).
3. Students who spend seven or more hours a day on social media score about 80 points lower in PISA mathematics than those who spend one to three.

**Proves.** When schools fail to build sustained reading and critical judgement, the platforms that capture students' attention become what they actually learn from, and those platforms reward engagement rather than accuracy.
**Stops.** The scores are correlations; screen time is a leading suspected cause, not a proven one. The decline is uneven: top performers are holding steady while the bottom falls away.

Aim it at:
- *‘Reading offers solutions to the pressing problems in the world.’* (2023 HCI). Reading cannot solve problems by itself, but a public that has lost the habit of sustained reading is less able to judge the solutions offered to it, which leaves it open to whoever simplifies most persuasively.
- *Do social media platforms facilitate or hinder constructive dialogue in your society?* (2023 ACJC). Social media hinders constructive dialogue when platforms reward the most provocative post rather than the most considered one, although Singapore's laws against online falsehoods and racial provocation set a floor that open platforms elsewhere lack.
- *‘Social media has changed who we admire today.’ To what extent does this bring more harm than good?* (2025 RVHS). Social media does harm when it lets people who capture attention appear to deserve admiration, since platforms rank creators by engagement, and engagement rewards outrage more readily than skill or integrity.

## Singapore in your society (`09`)

### A14. Nutri-Grade, and its beauty-industry parallel
**Hook:** the state redesigns the choice instead of banning it.

1. Singapore declared a War on Diabetes in 2016. Nutri-Grade A–D labels became mandatory on packaged drinks (30 December 2022) and freshly made drinks (30 December 2023), and the least healthy grade cannot be advertised. Sugary drinks remain legal.
2. Beauty was the most complained-about industry at the Consumers Association of Singapore (CASE) in 2025: 2,113 complaints, up 76.2%, with consumers losing more than $2.1 million, much of it in prepaid packages.
3. Abroad, France (from October 2017) and Norway (from July 2022) require a label on advertisements in which a model's body has been retouched, and England banned Botox and fillers for cosmetic use on under-18s in 2021.

**Proves.** A state can change behaviour without removing choice by redesigning what buyers see, which is easier to defend than a ban because adults remain free to choose.
**Stops.** A label changes what people see, not what they value. In beauty, the main local harm is money lost to prepaid packages, which is a contract problem that labels do not touch, and harm to self-image is harder to measure than a hospital bill.

Aim it at:
- *To what extent should the beauty industry be regulated?* (2022 TJC). The beauty industry should be regulated where its products or claims harm consumers who cannot judge them, through labels, limits on misleading advertising and protection for prepayments rather than bans on products adults choose freely.
- *How far should the state intervene in matters relating to one's body?* (2025 MI). The state is justified in intervening in bodily choices when their costs fall on a publicly funded health system, provided it shapes those choices through information and price rather than prohibition.
- *Should governments prioritise individual freedoms over the security of the country?* (2025 NYJC), as one comparison sentence. Governments can protect public welfare without sacrificing freedom when they change what citizens see rather than what they are allowed to do.

### A15. The trampoline and the Nordic net
**Hook:** Singapore helps those who can bounce; the most generous welfare states still cannot buy back births.

1. Then-Deputy Prime Minister Tharman (St Gallen, 2015; now President) described Singapore's welfare as a trampoline: help to study, work and own a home (SkillsFuture, Workfare). Silver Support and long-term ComCare are the net for those who can no longer work.
2. Singapore's resident fertility rate fell to 0.87 in 2025, and one in four citizens will be 65 or older by 2030.
3. The Nordic states spend over 3% of GDP on family support (long paid parental leave, cheap childcare), yet Finland's fertility rate fell to 1.25 in 2024, its lowest since records began in 1776, and Sweden's to a record 1.43. Most of the fall since 2010 comes from more people never having children.

**Proves.** Singapore gives second chances generously to people who can still work, because its welfare restores earning rather than replacing it. The Nordic comparison shows that even the most generous support changes the level of births but not their direction, because the decision to have children rests partly on things money cannot buy.
**Stops.** The trampoline fits gig workers, the disabled and the oldest-old poorly, and ageing adds every year to the group who cannot bounce. The Nordic net protects people who cannot work far better, so the comparison shows a trade-off, not a winner.

Aim it at:
- *Do you agree that people should always be given second chances?* (2025 ACJC). A society should offer second chances when the person can still rebuild, as Singapore's mid-career retraining schemes assume, though a chance that depends on the ability to work offers little to those whom age or illness has taken out of the labour market.
- *‘A society in which no one is left behind.’ To what extent is this an achievable goal for your society?* Singapore can keep working-age citizens from being left behind because its schemes restore their ability to earn, but an ageing population steadily enlarges the group a trampoline cannot help.
- *To what extent is there an ageing population crisis in your society?* Singapore faces a crisis of care rather than of planning, because the instruments it built years ahead assume a working life and a family able to care, and a fertility rate of 0.87 removes the carers; the Nordic states show that spending more would not, by itself, restore them.

### A16. Joseph Schooling
**Hook:** sport is valued as national pride, and as a career only for the exceptional few.

1. Rio 2016: Schooling won Singapore's first Olympic gold, in the 100m butterfly. His parents had spent over S$1 million on his training in the US.
2. MINDEF deferred his National Service from 2014 to 2021, a privilege it grants "very selectively" to athletes judged capable of winning international medals.
3. In 2022 he admitted using cannabis overseas while released from NS to compete, and MINDEF barred him from further leave to train or compete during his service. He retired in April 2024.

**Proves.** Singapore makes room for sport when it produces national pride, but only for the exceptional few when it competes with duty; for most families sport stays secondary to credentials because athletic careers are rare and uncertain.
**Stops.** One champion is not the whole system. Schools support sport through co-curricular activities and admission routes, and mass participation is a separate question from elite success.

Aim it at:
- *‘There is a lack of appreciation for the value of sport.’ How far is this true of your society?* (2026 EJC). Singapore appreciates sport when it produces national pride, as its willingness to defer an Olympic hopeful's National Service shows, but a pragmatic society that measures success through credentials still treats a sporting career as a risk few families will take.
- *‘Choosing an occupation should be about personal fulfilment, rather than the material prospects it brings.’ Is this good advice for your society?* (2022 DHS). Pursuing a vocation for fulfilment is realistic in Singapore mainly for families who can fund the risk, since the country's only Olympic champion needed over a million dollars of private support before the state invested in his talent.
- *Do you agree that people should always be given second chances?* (2025 ACJC). A society can offer a second chance without excusing the offence, as MINDEF did when it let Schooling complete his service but withdrew the privileges he had abused.
