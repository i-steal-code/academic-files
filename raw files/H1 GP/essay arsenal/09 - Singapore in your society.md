# Pillar: Singapore in your society (P1 and AQ)

**Engine:** Singapore is a resource-poor island that chose to live by trade, with a state strong enough to plan and a social compact that helps people bounce rather than be carried. Most “your society” answers turn on that constraint and that compact, not on a list of scheme names.

**Use this file when** the title says *in your society* / *your country*, or when AQ asks you to apply a passage to Singapore. A global case (US 2024, COVID) belongs in **one** sentence, as a pressure arriving in Singapore from outside, and a contrast (the Nordic states, South Korea) in two or three; then return to a feature below. The full rule for foreign examples is in `01` §5.4.

**Anchors:** A14 (Nutri-Grade and its beauty parallel), A15 (the trampoline and the Nordic net) and A16 (Joseph Schooling) in `02`, plus A10 (Circuit Breaker and the dormitories) from `07`. Section L sets out the ageing argument; section M the Nordic contrast; section K the consumer-protection facts for beauty titles.

**Weighting:** Dr Chan (AQ FAQs, Inner Guide) first. Budget and topic packs for proof. Half-weight notes only when they add and do not contradict.

## A. Features, not policies

Markers want to know **why Singapore is unlike the passage’s default society**. A policy name is proof. It is not the argument.

| Feature | What to understand | Proof (name one, then leave it) |
|---|---|---|
| **Resource-scarce island, open by design** | Little land, water or energy, and a long history as a trading port, forced a founding choice: stay useful to the world — a hub for ships, **foreign direct investment** and talent — or stagnate. Openness is how scarcity is paid for, not a separate personality. | HDB, NEWater, carbon tax, Coastal and Flood Protection Fund; EDB/FDI, foreign-worker levies and quotas |
| **High state capacity** | The government can design, fund and enforce at national scale, quickly. | Circuit Breaker, ComLink+, Ethnic Integration Policy in HDB estates |
| **Spend within means** | “No one owes Singapore a living.” Surpluses and reserves are treated as survival, not meanness. | GST, balanced-budget rule over the term of government, Assurance Package (support, not a welfare-state default) |
| **Multiracial compact** | Race and religion are managed as a security question in a small, dense city. Harmony in public is stronger than intimacy in private. | CMIO framing, racial-harmony education, IPS harmony surveys, PA integration work |
| **Meritocracy and pragmatism** | Worth is supposed to be demonstrated — exams, skills, delivery — rather than inherited or performed. Bread-and-butter outranks symbolism; elitism is the usual objection. | Streaming/pathways, SkillsFuture, Progressive Wage Model, Workfare |
| **Longevity without enough births** | People live long enough that old age is a mass condition, while too few children replace them. Care, tax and manpower strain follow from success, not from neglect alone. | CPF adjustments, Age Well SG, Silver Support, controlled immigration |
| **Self-reliance as a trampoline** | Help is meant to restore earning, learning and owning, not to replace family or effort. Those who cannot bounce (the oldest-old, severe disability) need something closer to a net — that is the standing limit. | HDB grants, Workfare, SkillsFuture, ComCare as last resort; Silver Support / long-term ComCare where work is no longer possible |
| **Technocratic trust gap** | The state often moves first and explains later. Citizens may accept competence and still resent being managed — especially on foreigners, cost of living, and speech. | POFMA, immigration controls, workplace-fairness legislation |

**Mechanism:** Because the island cannot retreat inland or live off a hinterland, every domestic choice is also a competitiveness choice and a cohesion choice. A policy looks generous or harsh depending on which of those two it is buying.

## A2. One-word labels in AQ: state the label, earn the link

**The rule.** Labels such as *fast-paced*, *open economy* or *meritocratic* can be stated without a source, because markers accept them as common ground about Singapore. Answer schemes do this: the 2025 TJC prelim scheme writes, “Given that a fast-paced and hectic life is the norm in Singapore, the convenience that online shopping offers is a lifesaver for time-strapped Singaporeans.” The marks go to the sentence that ties the label to the point. The 2024 VJC markers’ report found students repeating “fast-paced”, “competitive” and “meritocratic” across points; the traits were “valid, but they should be explained in a way that is relevant to the specific point and example,” and students should explain why a trend came about instead of only describing it.

**The tie-in, as one sentence.** Because of [label] — plus, when the answer needs depth, the root that makes Singapore this way — [which group] does or needs [what], so [the passage’s claim] holds, or fails, in Singapore, as [specific example] shows.

- ❌ “Singapore is a fast-paced society, so online shopping is beneficial.” The label is stated, but the sentence never says who is short of time or what online shopping changes for them.
- ✅ “Full-time employees in Singapore work about 45 hours a week (MOM, 2022), which leaves dual-income households little time for errands, so ordering groceries after the children are asleep gives families back time they did not have.”

**Five limits.**

1. **Only uncontroversial descriptions can go unsupported.** Evaluative or contested labels — *individualistic*, *materialistic*, *apathetic*, *authoritarian*, or *kiasu* applied to everyone — are claims, and need an example or a qualifier. Whenever the feature is itself the thing you are proving, support it the way Dr Chan’s FAQ supports “rapidly ageing”: with data, a named place or a policy.
2. **The consequence always needs an example.** “Singapore is fast-paced” can be stated; “so Singaporeans have no time for hobbies” is a claim that needs proof.
3. **A label fits some Singaporeans more than others.** *Fast-paced* describes working-age, dual-income households more than retirees. *Open economy* means opportunity for a professional with sought-after skills and competition for a mid-career worker whose job can be done more cheaply elsewhere. Saying whom the label fits is a stakeholder evaluation, which band 3–4 answers need.
4. **The same label can support either side.** The 2025 TJC scheme uses Singapore’s pace to argue that online convenience helps; the 2024 ACJC scheme uses the same pace to argue that hurried users overlook warning signs on gig platforms. The label does not decide your stand; the tie-in does. One label can therefore carry both an OV and its rebuttal.
5. **Labels are the surface, and “deeper Singapore context” is the root.** Singapore is fast-paced because an open economy competes for investment, a meritocratic system rewards credentials, and a land-scarce city is expensive to live in. Going one level down at least once per answer turns a label into an explanation. The roots are the features in table A.

**Reuse.** The same label may appear in more than one paragraph if the point differs, but explain it afresh for that point each time. Repeating the word without re-explaining it is what the VJC markers criticised.

| **Small, land-scarce, dense** | Every use of land competes with housing, industry and nature on a small island. | Claims about trade-offs in urban life are sharper here, because one more estate means one less forest or field. | Density is planned through HDB towns and park connectors, so crowding does not mean disorder. |

| **Affluent but expensive** | High incomes sit alongside high costs for housing, cars and healthcare, because land is scarce and the economy is open. | Claims about consumerism or cost-of-living pressure apply, because status goods and basic costs are both high. | National averages hide households in rental flats and the bottom fifth of earners. |
| **Family-centred, filial** | The family is meant to be the first source of care, written into housing grants and the Maintenance of Parents Act. | Claims about whether the state or the family should care for the old can be tested against a design that expects family first. | Families are shrinking and more seniors live alone, so family-first care fails exactly where it is most needed. |

## B. Nine facts

1. **Housing:** About **77%** of resident households live in **HDB** flats (2025); **owner-occupation** is about **91%**. Public housing is the main wealth and mixing tool, not a residual welfare estate.
2. **Island as hub:** With little of its own to sell from the ground, Singapore stays a trading and investment node. Global shocks (COVID, energy after Ukraine, US–China rivalry) reach Singapore as changes in prices, jobs and security risks.
3. **Ageing as the price of long life:** Life expectancy is among the world’s highest (about **85** at birth in recent Budget materials). **20.7%** of citizens were **65+** in June 2025; about **one in four** by **2030**. Longevity is a policy success that creates care demand.
4. **TFR:** The resident total fertility rate fell from **0.97** in 2024 to a record low of **0.87** in 2025. Births alone will not reverse ageing (Singstat / COS 2026).
5. **Harmony (IPS 2024, n=4,000):** **65.4%** rate racial and religious harmony high or very high; about **53%** have a close friend of another race. Acceptance is higher for colleagues than for in-laws.
6. **Workplace discrimination (IPS 2024):** **7%** reported discrimination over a job or promotion, down from **9.3%** in 2018 — better, not gone.
7. **Trampoline, not a Nordic net:** CPF ties retirement, housing and healthcare to work and saving. Then-DPM **Tharman** (St Gallen, 2015; now President): active support **for** personal and family responsibility — study, work, own a home — rather than support that takes those over.
8. **Forward Singapore (2022):** a restated compact — wider ideas of success, more collective responsibility, lifelong learning — without dropping the trampoline logic.
9. **2024 leadership and openness:** **Lawrence Wong** became PM on **15 May 2024** (**Lee Hsien Loong** became Senior Minister). On **6 May 2024**, still as DPM, Wong told *The Economist* that Singapore must stay open, with inflows **controlled and tiered** — otherwise citizens would be “swamped” — unlike the UAE’s oil-funded citizen-minority model.

## C. Same features, two papers

**Paper 1.** Mark which feature makes the global claim land differently. Two Singapore examples, then the principle. End on Insight → Qualifier → Implication, tied to a feature. For *effective / enough*, use SIRDEC before you praise a scheme. A one-word label from A2 is not enough in Paper 1: there is no passage for it to apply to, so the essay must show the feature with evidence. Use the table A feature, a fact from B and a pair from E, and let the label serve only as the one sentence naming which Singapore constraint changes the global claim.

**AQ.** No introduction. Name **Singapore** in the first line. A nuanced stand states a real concern **and** a judgement on it. SV: two local examples, layered so that both reveal the same underlying issue. OV + rebuttal, each with at least one example. Overseas material only if it sharpens Singapore. A2 labels may be stated without proof, but each one must be tied to the specific point and example, and taken down to its root at least once per answer.

## D. Topic forks

Write from the feature. Use the pair. Do not tour schemes.

### Politics, speech, media

In Singapore, public speech is treated less as a private right to be maximised than as a risk in a dense, multiracial city. The Public Order Act requires permits for most outdoor gatherings; in 2021, three of five people who protested outside MOE headquarters over discrimination against LGBTQ students and teachers were arrested for unlawful assembly. **POFMA** (in force October 2019) lets ministers order corrections of online falsehoods. The point is not that private prejudice disappears — IPS still finds residual workplace discrimination — but that falsehoods and foreign-funded campaigns are harder to turn into a communal incident.

**Pair:** POFMA / Public Order rules **with** Pink Dot 2017. After foreign entities were barred from Speakers’ Corner events, **120** local firms still raised **over $240,000**. Regulation can push a cause onto local ground; it does not erase the cause.

**Limit:** Success is deterrence and delay, not conversion. Views that cannot be aired in public simply move into private rooms.

### Economy, work, inequality, foreigners

Scarcity made openness a strategy: a port and a headquarters market, or a stagnant island. That is why foreign manpower is argued as arithmetic. In May 2024 Wong told *The Economist* that Singapore will not copy the UAE’s open-door citizen-minority model; inflows are **tiered** (work Singaporeans will not do; skills Singapore still lacks; and the crowded middle). After taking office he told students the same: firms locate regional operations here and will hire globally; shutting that door moves the operations, and the jobs, elsewhere. **Lee**, as SM (March 2025), called immigration “essential — even existential,” because there is no hinterland and not enough citizen births. Prefer Wong for a 2024-centred paper; Lee is usable as continuity, not as the main voice.

The other half of the bargain is the trampoline: **Workfare**, the **Progressive Wage Model** and **SkillsFuture** try to raise the local core rather than warehouse it. “Fewer foreigners” is only realistic where productivity and births close the gap. Until then the trade-off is competitiveness against crowding and identity.

**Pair:** Wong’s controlled openness (2024) **with** Workfare / PWM. Openness without support for local workers looks, to citizens, like being replaced by foreigners; support for local workers without openness cannot staff hospitals, shipyards or a regional business hub.

### Family, youth, the elderly

Tharman’s trampoline (2015) is still the design: help those who study, work and buy a home; make idleness unattractive; keep people proud of earning and raising a family. The family remains first; the state is not meant to become a parent. That design assumes people can still jump.

They increasingly cannot, at least not in the same numbers. The TFR of **0.87** (2025) and a citizen body heading for **one in four aged 65+** by 2030 mean old age is a mass condition after a long life, not a rare epilogue. **Age Well SG** — Toa Payoh first, then **Bedok, Bukit Panjang and Tiong Bahru–Redhill** (May 2026) — and a higher **Home Caregiving Grant** try to keep seniors in the community. CDC vouchers and baby bonuses ease a month’s bills; they do not, by themselves, reverse late marriage or the preference for fewer children. Material provision can be strong. Isolation, and the dignity of those who will never bounce again, are the harder test.

**Pair:** SkillsFuture / Workfare (bounce) **with** Silver Support or long-term ComCare (net). Same state, two instruments — because trampoline logic is a working-age rule with an old-age exception.

### Education, merit, discrimination

Education was how a poor island manufactured skill. It is still the main ladder: MOE assistance, **ComLink+** family coaches, mid-career **SkillsFuture**, ITE-to-polytechnic top-ups. The same ladder injures: tuition, brand-name schools, and the gap in esteem between “head” work and “hands” work. IPS 2024 found job-and-promotion discrimination down to **7%**, not to zero. A **Workplace Fairness** law, trailed in 2024 and passed in stages from 2025 (in force targeted for end-2027), tries to make merit real in hiring rather than only in exams.

**Pair:** ComLink+ (the state trying to equalise the start) **with** IPS workplace figures (private prejudice slower than public schemes). Meritocracy is both the official faith and the usual complaint.

### Environment, science, energy

A low-lying island that imports most of its fuel cannot treat climate policy as an optional lifestyle. The carbon tax is on a path toward **$50–80 per tonne by 2030**; coastal protection and a **Future Energy Fund** are engineering. Nuclear power has been studied since **2010** without a rush to build. **Budget 2026** added a **National AI Council** chaired by PM Wong — useful as a one-line proof that the state still bets on technology to stretch scarce labour; do not turn the Budget into the paragraph.

Daily habit is the weaker link. A target of **60,000** EV charging points by 2030 still leaves most of a large petrol fleet unmoved if charging is a nuisance. Protection is easier in flood walls than in consumption.

**Pair:** carbon tax / coastal funds **with** slow EV take-up. Capacity can price and build; it cannot, by itself, change what people will give up.

### Arts, culture, consumerism

A pragmatic, exam-driven, high-cost society is slow to treat the arts as necessary. Households under pressure will still rank tuition and a mortgage above a ticket. The state, even so, spends on arts and hawker culture as public identity — a shared culture for a mixed city, not only GDP. In the SG60 year a Culture Pass put a small sum in citizens’ hands for museums and performances; that is a nudge, not a conversion of household priorities.

**Pair:** national galleries, community arts or hawker heritage **with** tuition and property as status. Official value and household value are not the same; that gap *is* the “luxury” debate in Singapore.

### Security and the small state

There is no hinterland to fall back on. National Service and a serious defence budget are the price of remaining a sovereign trading island rather than a protected harbour. Diplomacy is not optional: wars and elections Singapore did not vote in still set energy prices, shipping risk, and the mood of a multiracial street. Neutrality is usefulness to several sides, not withdrawal.

**Pair:** NS / defence as the cost of sovereignty **with** higher prices after the Ukraine war, or tensions over overseas religious and ethnic conflicts spreading into Singapore. Singapore did not cause these crises, but it still bears their costs.

| | Ask |
|---|---|

| **I**ntent | Root or symptom? (vouchers vs why people do not marry) |

| **D**istribution | Who pays, who gains — poor vs middle, now vs later? |

## I. What costs marks

| Habit | Fix |
|---|---|
| Scheme names without a feature | State the Singapore constraint first, then use the policy as evidence of it |
| “Singapore is a developed country” | Too thin — name the specific feature: the resource-scarce island, the trampoline design of welfare, or the multiracial compact |
| AQ that tours the world | Singapore first |
| “The government always succeeds” | SIRDEC, then IPS or the group that cannot bounce |

| The same label repeated word for word in every paragraph | Re-explain it for each point, or use a different label whose root fits the new point better |
| A contested label stated as fact (“Singaporeans are materialistic / apathetic”) | Support it with an example, or limit it to a group (“many parents in the exam race…”) |
| A label applied to all Singaporeans | Name whom it fits and whom it misses; that sentence is your stakeholder evaluation |
| Only labels in a Paper 1 essay | Paper 1 needs evidence: a table A feature, a fact from B and a pair from E |

## L. The ageing argument: a crisis of care, not of planning

For "to what extent is there an ageing population crisis in your society?" and its cousins. Define *crisis* first: a structural mismatch that ordinary arrangements can no longer carry, not the mere fact that more people are old.

**The case against the word.** The state has prepared for decades. CPF ties retirement, housing and healthcare to work; about 91% of resident households own their homes, so many older Singaporeans hold an asset. Silver Support pays a cash supplement to the elderly poor. Age Well SG has begun refitting neighbourhoods (Toa Payoh first, then Bedok, Bukit Panjang and Tiong Bahru–Redhill from May 2026) so that care sits nearer the flat. SM Lee called immigration "essential, even existential" in March 2025 because citizen births cannot fill the gap.

**Why preparation does not close the mismatch.** Every instrument assumes something Singapore no longer has enough of. CPF assumes a working life with an employer. Silver Support and Age Well SG assume a family, or a service, able to do the daily work of care. Immigration assumes a public willing to admit the numbers required, and Wong's insistence in May 2024 that inflows stay tiered so citizens are not "swamped" shows the political ceiling. A fertility rate of 0.87 in 2025 removes the carers of the 2030s, when about one in four citizens will be 65 or older. Baby bonuses change the cost of a month; they do not restore the children who were not born.

**Who it falls on.** The crisis is sharpest for those who cannot bounce: the oldest, the childless, seniors living alone, and those with thin savings or interrupted careers who never received the employer contribution CPF assumes. The Maintenance of Parents Act shows that the state's first answer is still the adult child, who often works about 45 hours a week, so care is hired out to a migrant domestic worker with little protection. The working-age generation pays twice: into its own retirement and for its parents' care. A homeowner with a full career and children nearby is not in crisis.

**Where the word stops.** It does not mean the state was caught by surprise, or that long life is a failure. Reserves and the rule of spending within means give the state time; the crisis is the decade in which a quarter of citizens are old while births stay below one child per woman.

**Endings that fit.** Trade-off: Singapore gained years of life and lost the brothers and sisters who would have provided care. Reversal: a crisis the government planned for years ahead and still left care missing is a choice, which is a harder charge than an accident. Yardstick: Age Well SG succeeds as a service and fails as a remedy for the shortage of carers (`01` §6.4).

## M. The Nordic contrast (A15)

**Facts.** The Nordic states spend over 3% of GDP on family support, mainly through long paid parental leave and cheap childcare. Yet Finland's total fertility rate fell to 1.25 in 2024, its lowest since records began in 1776; Sweden's fell to a record low of 1.43 and Norway's was 1.44. Nordic demographers attribute most of the fall since 2010 to more people never having children, especially women under 30.

**What it adds to a Singapore essay.** It stops the essay from listing baby bonuses and assuming that more money would work. Singapore, with less generous family support, is at 0.87; the Nordic states, with the most generous support in the world, are around 1.4. Policy changes the level of births, not the direction, because the decision to have children rests partly on things money cannot buy: time, the cost of a child who can compete, and changing ideas of a good life.

**Where it fits.** Ageing and fertility titles, as the contrast that tests Singapore's incentives; titles on the limits of state power, alongside A6 (a state cannot legislate desire); family, gender and work-life titles. Use two or three sentences, inside a paragraph built on Singapore.

**Where it stops.** The Nordic net protects those who cannot work far better than Singapore's trampoline, so the contrast shows a trade-off between designs, not that Singapore's is superior.
