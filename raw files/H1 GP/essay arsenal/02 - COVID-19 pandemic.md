# Case 2 — COVID-19 (a test of every sector at once)

**Engine:** COVID-19 was not just a health crisis with social side effects. It tested several systems at the same time: science, the state, the economy, households, and public trust in information.

**Use for:** almost any title — but only if you say **which system failed and how**. “COVID changed everything” scores nothing.

**In 2026:** do not retell 2020. Use a mechanism that still holds, and end on one of the pandemic’s lasting after-effects: learning loss, inflation, remote work, or political polarisation.

**Memorise:** 9 facts, 1 mechanism, **3 pairs**. Forks are lookup.

---

## A. Plot

Early 2020: a new coronavirus spread into a pandemic. Governments had to choose between bad options: close businesses and schools to keep hospitals from being overwhelmed, or stay open and risk hospitals collapsing. **BioNTech/Pfizer** and **Moderna** mRNA vaccines arrived within a year (BioNTech developed the first; Pfizer manufactured and distributed it). WHO ended the international health emergency in 2023. The social and economic costs did not end with it.

COVID exposed dependencies that societies had ignored:
- supply chains that kept almost no spare stock (“just-in-time”), so a single disruption caused shortages;
- essential care work that was poorly paid;
- working parents’ reliance on schools for childcare;
- migrant workers housed in crowded dormitories;
- scientific experts who suddenly needed the whole public to trust them.

**Mechanism:** A fast-spreading airborne virus forces governments to make trade-offs in public. Inequalities that already existed — in class, age, housing, internet access and trust in government — decide who bears the cost. Scientists can produce a vaccine faster than a society can agree on whether to trust it and who should get it first.

---

## B. Nine facts

1. Pandemic declared 2020; WHO ended the international emergency in 2023. No death total needed.
2. Core trade-off: protecting lives and hospital capacity versus protecting jobs, incomes and personal freedom.
3. mRNA vaccines (BioNTech/Pfizer and Moderna) were developed in under a year; public trust in them did not grow as fast.
4. Supply chains broke down (protective equipment, chips, shipping). Because just-in-time supply kept no spare stock, shortages followed, and inflation followed the shortages.
5. School closures caused learning loss, worsened students’ mental health, and left working parents without childcare.
6. Inequality: professionals could work from home online; essential workers, including Singapore’s migrant workers, kept working in person and caught the virus.
7. Singapore: Circuit Breaker (Apr 2020); TraceTogether/SafeEntry contact tracing; Jobs Support Scheme; later the shift to “living with COVID.”
8. Singapore’s most revealing case: the 2020 outbreak in foreign-worker dormitories showed that infection risk followed where people lived, and where people lived followed their class and job.
9. Afterwards: governments’ emergency powers, fights over mandates, conspiracy theories and distrust of experts carried over into the polarised politics of the 2020s.

---

## C. Three pairs (memorise these)

| Pair | EG1 | EG2 | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Housing and class decided who got infected | Singapore’s migrant workers, housed in crowded dormitories, were infected in large numbers | Professionals worked safely from home over Zoom | The same virus hit people differently according to their housing and work — **so long as** an economy keeps low-wage labour cheap by housing it densely, those workers carry the risk |
| Science moved faster than trust | mRNA vaccines were developed at record speed | Many well-educated people still refused vaccination | Scientific tools can be produced quickly, but public trust cannot — **especially when** officials speak with more certainty than the evidence supports and later have to reverse themselves |
| Lasting after-effects | Stimulus spending combined with supply bottlenecks caused inflation | Fights over mandates hardened into tribal politics | The emergency left behind distrust of authority and anger over living costs that persisted **after** the virus receded |

Do not retell 2020. End on a lasting after-effect, or on an inequality the pandemic exposed.

## D. Forks (one point each)

| Topic | Land here |
|---|---|
| Health / science | mRNA vaccines came from decades of earlier research plus massive emergency funding, not from a sudden breakthrough. Deciding who receives scarce vaccines first is an ethical question, not a scientific one. Trust the scientific method, not every official announcement. |
| Government / liberty | The pandemic showed that states can still control what citizens do with their bodies, through lockdowns and mandates. TraceTogether showed that a government can use data competently and still raise legitimate questions about privacy. China’s zero-COVID policy, followed by its sudden abandonment in 2022, showed that a state able to enforce strict rules can still fail if it cannot adjust them as conditions change. The distrust that followed is part of the policy’s cost. |
| Economy | Professionals working from home kept their incomes and their health; workers who had to turn up in person were exposed to infection. Stimulus spending plus supply bottlenecks caused the high prices of 2022–24. The crisis sped up digital platforms that were already growing. Singapore: the Jobs Support Scheme paid part of workers’ wages so firms kept them employed; the dormitory outbreaks showed that the economy’s growth relied on housing low-wage workers in crowded conditions. |
| Education / youth | Home-based learning depended on whether parents could supervise and whether the home had reliable internet, so it widened gaps between families. Schools also provide friendship and social development, which cannot happen over Zoom. |
| Elderly / family | The elderly died at far higher rates, and isolating them was official policy. Contact-tracing apps disadvantaged elderly people without smartphones or digital skills. Singapore: public praise of filial piety sat alongside a heavy reliance on migrant domestic workers to do the actual caring. |
| Media / truth | The “infodemic” was a flood of false and contradictory information. There were two failures: conspiracy theories, and official overconfidence that later had to be reversed (on airborne spread, on masks). While the science is still changing, institutions must explain their reasoning and their uncertainty. |
| Tech | The pandemic sped up the adoption of telemedicine, remote work and e-commerce. The downside: more screen time, larger tech monopolies, and the risk that contact-tracing data is used for other purposes. The real question is how long that data is kept and who can access it, not whether the apps work. |
| Arts | Live performance was the first sector to shut and the last to reopen. Calling it “non-essential” was a budgeting category, not a judgement of its value to people. |
| Environment | Emissions fell in 2020, then rebounded. Lockdown is not a climate policy, because societies will not accept shutting down the economy to cut emissions. Skip anecdotes about wildlife returning to cities. |
| IR / globalisation | Rich countries secured vaccines for themselves first; COVAX, the scheme to share vaccines with poorer countries, was a late and partial correction. Viruses cross borders regardless of sovereignty. Moving supply chains to friendly countries (“friend-shoring”) after COVID was an early form of the economic decoupling that later wars sped up. Singapore: as a trade and travel hub, it came to a standstill when borders closed. |
| Ethics | Personal liberty versus the duty not to infect others. School closures protected grandparents but harmed pupils — a genuine trade-off between two goods. COVID showed which groups society had already accepted living in crowded conditions. |
| Singapore (“your society”) | Circuit Breaker showed a state able to shut down a whole city. TraceTogether showed a state able to turn public-health science into a working system quickly. The dormitory outbreaks showed that economic growth relied on housing migrant workers in crowded conditions the public had chosen not to look at. Pair the first two with the third, or the paragraph reads as government publicity. |

## E. Link to US 2024

COVID taught many voters to be suspicious of experts, of foreigners, and of any policy justified as being “for your own good.” In 2024 that suspicion shaped how Americans voted, while their government was still funding two wars.

---

## F. Exam mistakes

- “COVID showed health matters.” Says nothing.
- Celebrating scientists as heroes while ignoring who got the vaccine, who trusted it, and which classes bore the risk.
- Using only Western lockdowns.
- Comparing death tolls between countries as if the numbers were the argument.
- Ending in 2020. End on learning loss, inflation, hybrid work, or polarisation.
