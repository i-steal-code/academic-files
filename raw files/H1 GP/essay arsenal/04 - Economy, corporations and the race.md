# Pillar: Economy, corporations and the race

**Engine:** Two forces run through this pillar. The first is war transmitted through money: the Iran war closed the Strait of Hormuz and raised oil prices, which pushed up inflation in oil-importing countries; the US central bank raised interest rates; and higher rates hit AI companies hardest, because their share prices rest on profits expected years from now and their data-centre build-out is increasingly paid for with borrowed money. The October 2026 chip selloff is therefore a repricing of how AI is financed, not evidence that demand for AI chips has collapsed (sections A–F). The second is involution: races in which every player, from a young office worker to a central government, can see the waste and none can afford to stop first (sections G–H).

**Use for:** economy, globalisation, technology and hype, science and progress, inequality (who pays), war and its costs, small states, Singapore's open economy, competition, corporations against governments, work and youth, hard work and success. For the political side of the same wars, see `03` section G.

**Anchors:** A4 (Hormuz), A5 (TSMC and CoreWeave) and A6 (China's involution) in `02`; the rest of this file is depth. All market figures are as of 17 October 2026 and will move; quote them as "in October 2026".

## A. Plot

On 28 February 2026 the US and Israel struck Iran. Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, and oil prices jumped. By September, American petrol was a third more expensive than a year earlier, and inflation was above forecasts for the second month running. The Federal Reserve raised interest rates in September for the first time since 2023, and US government bond yields climbed above 5%.

In mid-October investors turned on the AI trade. Chip shares fell for eight sessions in a row, and the cost of insuring the debt of heavily borrowed AI firms reached record highs. CoreWeave, a loss-making company that rents out Nvidia chips and owes about $50 billion, became the symbol of the worry. Over the same week TSMC, which manufactures most of the world’s advanced AI chips, reported record profits. Chip orders kept growing while confidence in the borrowing behind the AI build-out fell.

**Mechanism:** A war that disrupts energy raises prices far from the battlefield. Central banks fight that inflation by raising interest rates. Higher rates reduce what investors will pay today for profits expected far in the future, and they raise the cost of borrowing. AI companies suffer on both counts, because they are valued on distant profits and are increasingly building with debt. So the war’s main route into the AI industry is the cost of money, not the supply of chips.

---

## B. Nine facts

1. **Oil shock.** After the strikes of 28 February 2026, Iran effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, which normally carries about a fifth of the world’s oil. Brent crude rose from about $72 a barrel to nearly $120 at its peak.
2. **US inflation.** In September 2026, US consumer prices were 3.7% higher than a year earlier. Petrol was up 34% and heating oil 74%; producer prices rose 6.3%.
3. **Interest rates.** The Federal Reserve, chaired by Kevin Warsh, raised rates in September 2026 for the first time since 2023, and most officials expect another rise this year. US Treasury yields rose above 5%, and the 30-year mortgage rate reached 7.56%.
4. **The selloff.** The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell more than 13% in seven sessions in October, erasing about $2 trillion in market value; the losing streak matched its longest since the 2008 financial crisis. Nvidia fell more than 11% below its 52-week high. The index was still up nearly 60% for 2026.
5. **The weakest borrower.** CoreWeave owes about $50 billion and is still losing money. The cost of insuring its debt passed 11% a year, which markets read as about a 64% chance of default within five years, up from about 30% a year earlier. Nvidia has agreed to buy any computing capacity CoreWeave cannot sell until 2032, so a chipmaker is guaranteeing demand from one of its own customers.
6. **Debt replacing cash.** The cost of insuring Amazon, Alphabet and Meta debt also reached record highs, though it rose far more slowly. Broadcom’s chief executive, Hock Tan, warned that financing could become a bigger limit on AI expansion than shortages of chips or electricity.
7. **Demand has not collapsed.** TSMC reported its sixth consecutive quarter of record profit and forecast fourth-quarter revenue 58% higher than a year earlier.
8. **Gulf money under strain.** The war cut Gulf states’ energy and tourism income, and Moody’s moved Bahrain’s outlook to negative in April. The Council on Foreign Relations argues that Gulf sovereign funds, major investors in American AI, may pull back, which would push AI firms to borrow more. This is an analyst’s argument, not an observed withdrawal.
9. **Singapore.** The economy grew 5.4% year on year in the third quarter of 2026, and the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) says technology-related industries produced about three-quarters of that growth; oil-related industries were still contracting. Imported energy and food pushed core inflation to 2.1% in July–August. On 14 October MAS kept the Singapore dollar on its rising path, which makes imports cheaper, and named two risks to growth: a sharp rise in global interest rates, or a slowdown in global AI investment.

## C. Why the distinction matters

Do not write that the AI bubble has burst or that chip demand has collapsed. The precise claim is that **the technology is selling, and the borrowing behind it is being repriced**. Companies are still buying chips at record rates. What investors now doubt is whether the firms that borrowed to buy those chips will earn enough, soon enough, to repay the debt at today’s higher interest rates. The losses therefore fall first on the most indebted firms and on the people who lent to them, not on the people who use AI.

## F. Terminal (Insight → Qualifier → Implication)

> The 2026 AI selloff tests how AI is financed rather than whether the technology works, **because** companies borrowed against distant profits that higher interest rates now make worth less, **so long as** chip orders keep rising as TSMC’s do; **therefore** the losses fall first on the most indebted firms and their lenders, and an economy like Singapore’s is exposed through its exports long before its households feel any change in how they use AI.

## G. Lying flat: the race seen from below

**The post.** In April 2021 Luo Huazhong, then 31 and a former factory worker, posted "Lying flat is justice" (躺平即是正义) on a Baidu forum. He wrote that he had not worked for more than two years and did not think this was wrong; that pressure came from people ranking one another and from the older generation's idea of a proper life; and that he lived on about 200 yuan a month, working one or two months a year. He compared himself to Diogenes sleeping in a barrel: "Only by lying flat is man the measure of all things." The post was deleted, but the phrase spread.

**What others made of it.** The people reporters interviewed were still employed. They used "lying flat" for something milder: doing the job but refusing overtime, promotion and the expected sequence of a flat, a car, marriage and a child. Online groups numbering from about 6,000 to "upwards of 200,000" members were reported and then deleted; these are counts of forum members that contradict one another, not a census of people who quit work. In December 2021 a Ministry of Education research centre listed "lying flat" among the year's ten most popular internet memes.

**The conditions it named.** The "996" schedule (9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week) had been defended in China's technology sector; Jack Ma called Alibaba's hours a blessing. On 27 August 2021 the Supreme People's Court and the labour ministry declared 996 a serious violation of legal working hours, which did not end the practice. City housing prices had outrun what a degree could pay for. And on 31 May 2021, weeks after the post spread, the state allowed married couples a third child, asking the same generation to do the opposite of the meme's "do not have children".

**The state's answer.** On 20 May 2021 *Nanfang Daily* called lying flat "shameful" and Xinhua reposted the commentary; censors deleted the post, closed the main Douban group and restricted the hashtag. In October 2021 Xi Jinping called for avoiding both involution and lying flat. In August 2023, after the urban unemployment rate for 16-to-24-year-olds reached 21.3% in June, the statistics bureau stopped publishing the series; it resumed in January 2024 excluding students, at 14.9% for December 2023. The two numbers measure different populations, so the drop is a change of method, not an improvement. In September 2025 the Cyberspace Administration opened a campaign against content that "maliciously incites negative emotions", and on 28 April 2026 the Ministry of State Security described the encouragement to lie flat as ideological infiltration by hostile foreign forces.

**What it proves.** Effort is a bargain, not a virtue. Young people work hard while hard work buys a home, security and respect; when the price of those rises faster than effort can pay, they stop competing, and where open protest is closed off they withdraw quietly, which is the one refusal a state cannot easily punish. Huang Ping, who studies youth culture at East China Normal University, put the sequence plainly: when conditions are tolerable people feel involuted but keep trying, and when they cannot keep up, lying flat is the rational choice.

**Where it stops.** Most people who "lie flat" still work, so a topic sentence claiming that Chinese youth have abandoned work misreads the case. Lying flat is easier for those with fewer obligations: Luo had no mortgage, no dependants and rural costs, which an urban graduate supporting parents does not. China's falling birth rate predates the meme, which gave the refusal a slogan rather than causing it.

**Transfer.** The same refusal recurs wherever housing and credentials outrun wages: South Korea's young people who give up dating, marriage and children because of cost, Japan's adults in part-time work, and America's "quiet quitting" from 2022. Only the state's tolerance of it varies, which points to a structural cause rather than a national character flaw.

**Singapore fork.** Do not write that Singaporean youth are lying flat; no evidence supports it. Singapore shares the conditions (exam competition, high housing costs, success measured by credentials and property) but answers frustration with policy rather than censorship: Forward Singapore promised wider definitions of success, and the Tripartite Guidelines on Flexible Work Arrangement Requests, in force from 1 December 2024, require employers to consider requests to work flexibly properly. A resident fertility rate of 0.87 in 2025 shows young Singaporeans making the same private calculation about children without any slogan.

## H. Involution from the top: the race the powerful cannot leave

Most accounts of involution describe exhausted individuals. Seen from the top, it is a race the people in charge can see clearly and still cannot leave, because whoever stops first loses ground to whoever keeps going. Their frustration is on record. What separates them from the masses is not that they lose nothing, but that they can push most of the loss onto someone else.

**The structure.** Involution is a race in which each player's sensible choice adds up to an outcome all of them would rather avoid. A firm cuts prices because its rivals will; a province subsidises a factory because the next province will; a technology giant overbuilds because falling behind looks worse than wasting money. No player can end it by stopping alone. Only a referee that binds every player at once can, and at the top the referee is often itself a player, or the largest player refuses to be bound.

**China's central government.** On 1 July 2025 Xi Jinping told the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission that "disorderly low-price competition" among enterprises must be regulated. By then factory-gate prices had fallen for 33 months in a row. The International Monetary Fund's 2025 review of China traces the overinvestment in steel, solar panels, electric vehicles and batteries partly to local governments' industrial policy, and judged that the campaign alone was unlikely to do much about deflation. In 2021 Beijing censored the mood of the young; in 2025 it tried to regulate the behaviour of firms. Both times it treated the response to the race rather than the incentives that drive it, incentives its own growth targets had created.

**Local governments.** Provincial and city officials compete for factories, jobs and tax revenue, and their careers depend on growth. An official who stops subsidising a solar or car plant does not end the overcapacity; the factory moves to the next province, and the official's record goes with it.

**Chinese carmakers.** In May 2025 BYD cut the starting price of its cheapest model, the Seagull, to 55,800 yuan. Great Wall Motor's chairman, Wei Jianjun, called the industry "unhealthy" and warned that the car industry's own "Evergrande" (the property developer whose collapse began China's housing crisis) already existed but had not yet collapsed; a BYD executive called him alarmist. The losses moved down the supply chain. In June 2025, after steelmakers protested how long they waited to be paid and under regulatory pressure, 17 carmakers including BYD, Geely and Chery pledged to pay suppliers within 60 days.

**American technology giants.** In July 2024 Sundar Pichai told investors that for Alphabet "the risk of under-investing is dramatically greater than the risk of over-investing." In September 2025 Mark Zuckerberg said that if Meta ended up "misspending a couple of hundred billion dollars" that would be unfortunate, "but I actually think the risk is higher on the other side", and called an AI bubble "quite possible". These executives are not confident; they have calculated that being caught behind is worse than being wrong. If they are wrong, shareholders, lenders and heavily indebted firms such as CoreWeave (A5) pay first.

**States competing for capital.** Governments race to attract multinational companies, and for decades they did it by cutting corporate tax. In 2021 more than 130 countries agreed, through the OECD and G20, a 15% global minimum tax to stop that race. In June 2025 the G7 agreed a "side-by-side" system exempting US-parented companies from key parts of the rules, after the US dropped Section 899, a proposed retaliatory tax on foreign companies, from its budget bill. Singapore applied the 15% top-up tax from 2025 and, in Budget 2024, introduced Refundable Investment Credits that reward investment in productive capacity, research and decarbonisation. Once the rate was capped, competition moved to incentives, the same pattern as an exam reform that leaves the selection gate in place (A12). A small state that depends on foreign investment cannot simply stop competing, because the investment would go elsewhere.

**Governments against their own most powerful families.** In June 2023 South Korea's President Yoon ordered the national entrance exam to test only what schools teach, and the education ministry identified 22 "killer questions" to remove. In 2024 private education spending reached a record 29.2 trillion won, up 7.7%, with 80,000 fewer students, and the richest households spent the most. The state could change the exam but not stop the families with the most money from finding the next edge.

**The winners themselves.** Daniel Markovits argues in *The Meritocracy Trap* (2019) that meritocracy also harms those at the top: elite professionals work punishing hours and elite children are trained relentlessly, because status must be re-earned in every generation. Their losses are time, health and family life rather than livelihood, which is why their exhaustion is rarely seen as part of the same problem.

**What the powerful cannot push down.** Most of their financial losses pass downward: carmakers' price cuts become suppliers' delayed payments; technology companies' overbuilding becomes losses for shareholders and lenders; local subsidies become debt for future taxpayers; elite families' spending raises the price of competing for everyone below them. Three costs stay at the top. **Control:** Beijing can declare 996 illegal and order an end to price wars, but it cannot command prices, effort or births. **Legitimacy:** each time a power player blames the people running the race instead of its rules, as with "lying flat is shameful" and the 2026 security video, it spends credibility it cannot easily rebuild. **Time:** for the elite individual, the hours, health and childhood spent preparing cannot be delegated.

**What ends the race.** A race stops only when a referee binds every competitor at once. It works best with few players and an enforceable rule: the Montreal Protocol had few producers, existing substitutes and a fund that paid poorer countries to comply (A7). It weakens when the largest player can opt out, as the global minimum tax did after the US exemption. It fails when the referee is also a player, as when Beijing asks local governments to stop a race its own targets rewarded. And it fails when the rule misses the real prize, as when Korea removed hard exam questions while university prestige still decided futures.

**Pairs.**

| Pair | EG1 | EG2 | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| The race from both ends | Young Chinese workers who stopped trying harder in 2021 because overtime no longer bought a home | Chinese carmakers who kept cutting prices in 2025 while their own chairmen warned of collapse | The same race produces withdrawal at the bottom and escalation at the top, because the powerful can push their losses down while the weak can only stop taking part |
| A referee that binds, and one that bends | The 2021 global minimum tax curbed a decades-long race to cut corporate tax | The 2025 G7 exemption for US-parented firms let the largest player step outside it | A rule ends a race only while every major competitor is bound, so the referee is only as strong as the largest player's willingness to accept it |

**Topic sentences.**

- *‘Corporations, rather than governments, shape the world.’* Corporations appear to shape the world when governments compete for their investment, but that competition is itself a race between governments, which shows that firms gain most when states refuse to bind themselves together.
- *‘Competition does more harm than good.’ How true is this of your society?* (2025 EJC). Competition does more harm than good when every competitor can see the waste and none can afford to stop first, as even powerful players, from Chinese carmakers to American technology giants, admit.
- *Given the competition for technological dominance, how realistic is international cooperation?* (2026 ASRJC). Cooperation is realistic only when it binds every competitor at once; the global minimum tax curbed a race to cut corporate tax and then weakened once the largest economy secured an exemption.

## I. Exam mistakes

- Writing “the AI bubble has burst.” The chip index was still up nearly 60% for 2026, and TSMC’s orders were at a record.
- Writing that chip demand collapsed. Share prices and borrowing costs fell; chip sales did not.
- Reading the 64% figure as a forecast. It is the default risk implied by what markets charge to insure CoreWeave’s debt, not a prediction by any analyst.
- Writing that Gulf funds have withdrawn from American AI. The Council on Foreign Relations describes a risk, not an event.
- Treating one week’s prices as permanent. Date every figure as October 2026.
- Blaming the selloff on the war alone. Interest rates were the main trigger, but competition in memory chips and doubts about debt-funded spending also weighed on prices.
- Calling lying flat a movement with members. It is a phrase and a stance; the group counts are of deleted forums and contradict one another.
- Treating "Chinese youth" as one group. Name who lies flat and why: low-paid workers in the 996 industries, and graduates priced out of city housing.
- Comparing the 21.3% and 14.9% youth unemployment figures as a fall. They measure different populations.
- Writing that the powerful lose nothing. They push financial losses down, but lose control, legitimacy and time.
- Ending on "China is authoritarian". That answers no title; end on what the case shows about effort, reward and who can leave a race.
