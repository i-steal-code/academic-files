# Essay craft (Paper 1, with AQ notes)

This is the single standard for how an essay is chosen, planned and written. It replaces the house model, the so-what notes, the openers in the old crosswalk, and the two planning references (SV and R reference; Insights from Q1–Q2). The anchors in `02` and the pillars in `03`–`09` supply content; this file decides what is done with it.

Sources, in order of weight: Dr Chan's October 2026 notes (*Why Essays are in B3 Content*, *Evaluating Two Examples in an SV Paragraph*, *Strengthening EV and CU with One Example*, *Content Update: Mass Media, Politics and Governance*); the Inner Guide, P1 notes and J2 Thinking Routines; the 8881 syllabus band descriptors; Cambridge and school markers' reports (§9).

---

## 1. The ninety minutes

| Phase | Minutes | What happens |
|---|---:|---|
| Select | 3 | Read all eight titles, test two or three against the selection rule below, and commit to one |
| Unpack | 2 | Run SALT, the question angle and the keyword traps (§2) on the chosen title |
| Introduction | 8 | Write it straight onto the script, because the stand is already formed |
| OV | 15 | Write it straight; note in the margin which absolute word R will attack |
| Plan R, SV1, SV2 | 20 | Use the planning sheet (§7) only for these three blocks |
| Write R | 8 | Keep it to half the length of one SV |
| Write SV1 and SV2 | 24 | Twelve minutes each, using the seven-sentence skeleton (§3) |
| Conclusion | 5 | Restate the stand and give the moral of the story; no new example |
| Check | 5 | Keyword in every topic sentence and every last line |

**The hybrid rule.** The introduction and the OV can be written straight because their logic is usually clear once the title is chosen. R and the SVs cannot, because unplanned R and SV paragraphs are where chaining and missing terminals come back. If the unpacking stalls, plan the whole essay on the sheet first and take the extra time from the check and from SV2, never from the OV or R. The twenty-minute planning block is protected.

**The selection rule (three minutes).** Choose a title only if you can name three things for it: the trap in its keyword, an anchor whose "proves" line answers that keyword, and a counter-example that would break the opposing view. Value titles with no anchor (fantasy fiction, travel, a single world language) are worth choosing only when the value argument (§6.5) is already clear in your head.

**Default cross-outs.** Science-and-technology and media titles (about 18% of prelim questions) are crossed out at the start. Most of the cohort writes them, and the generic answers they invite are where Band 3 essays cluster, so the selection minutes go to titles where the anchors supply an argument most candidates do not have. Reopen one only when it is clearly the best title on the paper: it passes the selection rule, and its real question is who controls a technology or who pays for it, where A5, A8, A9 and `06` §C are strong.

**Length.** Length is not a target; it is what an essay comes to once every block has done its job. Dr Chan's guidance is that model essays run up to about 1,100 words, and the shortest, at just over 900, barely have room for examples. The question paper's 500–800 guide describes a minimum answer, not what Band 4–5 scripts look like. A complete essay on this model comes to about 950 words: introduction about 120, OV about 180, R about 100, each SV about 200, conclusion about 100. Well under 900 usually means a block has lost something the descriptors pay for, most often the comparison of two examples or the qualifier; well over 1,100 usually means an example is being retold. The real limit is the hand: the clock leaves about sixty minutes of writing, so 950 words is about sixteen words a minute, which is why fact lines stay short and the analysis carries the length.

## 2. Unpacking the title

**SALT.** Name the stakeholders (who is helped, who is hurt and who decides), the aspects (economic, political, social, cultural, technological, environmental, ethical), the levels (individual, community, national, global) and the time frame (past against present, short run against long run, and what the words *still*, *no longer* and *today* imply).

**Question angle** (Inner Guide). Decide whether the title asks about value, effectiveness, responsibility, possibility, regulation or justification, because each angle needs a different yardstick.

**Keyword traps.** The words *still, only, always, too, nothing more than, more than, rather than, should, really, enough, key* and *inevitable* must appear in topic sentences and last lines, not only in the introduction. Do not shift the goalposts: an essay on "welcome migrants" that argues only about admitting workers has answered a different question.

| Question type | What the essay must do |
|---|---|
| To what extent, how far | Grant the claim real merit, then show the conditions under which it stops holding. A limit is a condition; a single exception is not a limit. |
| Absolute (*always, only, nothing more than*) | Give the merit of the assumption, then refute the absolute word itself. |
| Effectiveness, *enough* | State a yardstick first: awareness, then behaviour change, then sustained change, then alleviation of the problem. GP has no "solutions", so judge a policy against the yardstick instead of listing measures. |
| Comparative (*A rather than B*, *more curse than blessing*) | Compare like with like on the same scale, impact and time (SIT), and ask whether A acts independently of B or through B. |
| Your society | Argue from a Singapore feature (`09` §A), not from a list of scheme names. |

## 3. Stand and architecture

**Introduction (four jobs).** Show why the debate matters now, give both perspectives, define and scope the keywords, and state a nuanced stand. A nuanced stand names a condition: "Corporations shape the world where governments compete for their investment, but governments still set the terms when they act together." "I agree to a large extent" is a degree, not a stand, and ACJC's 2024 markers flagged it as false nuance.

**What each block owns.**

| Block | Its job | What it must not do |
|---|---|---|
| OV | Gives the strongest case for the claim, with real examples, so it is not a straw man | It does not hint at your rebuttal halfway through |
| R | Breaks the absolute or total word in the OV with one named counter-example and a line of logic | It does not build your own case, add a second example, or run to SV length |
| SV1 | Builds the first layer of your case around a new noun, with two examples and a terminal | It does not repeat the rebuttal's point with more evidence |
| SV2 | Builds a second layer around a different noun, with balance inside the paragraph | It does not reuse an example from R or SV1 |
| Conclusion | Restates the stand, adds value and ends on the moral of the story | It does not introduce a new argument or example |

**The rebuttal (80–100 words, four sentences).** Concede the OV's partial truth in a clause; name what its absolute word wrongly assumes; give the counter-example (who, what, when); finish with the logic that makes the total claim fail. Test the counter-example by asking whether the OV would still look totally true without it. If it would, the example belongs in R. If the example instead builds your own positive theory, it belongs in SV1. The prelim migrants essay broke this rule: its rebuttal used a different country and a different kind of migrant, so it read as an extra SV rather than an attack on the OV's logic.

**The SV skeleton (about 200 words, seven sentences).**

1. Topic sentence: the new noun plus the title's keyword, in conditional form (§4).
2. EG1 in one line: who, what, when.
3. EG2 in one line.
4. Comparison: the principle the pair shows that neither shows alone (§5.1).
5. *Because*: the mechanism.
6. *So long as*, *for whom* or *when*: the qualifier.
7. *Therefore*: the terminal, landing on the title's keyword (§6).

Keep two lines of fact to five of analysis. Give each SV a different noun (delivery, institutions, identity, cost, rules), and apply the chain test: if SV1 could begin "Furthermore…" straight after R and mean the same thing, rewrite it.

**Finding two SVs that do not overlap.** Split the title's noun into layers (who decides against who pays; the technology against its financing), or split by stakeholder or timescale. Then ask what would be impossible if the OV were totally true. That impossibility is SV1; a second one is SV2.

**Balance inside SVs.** Band 4 and above needs evaluation inside each SV (a context, stakeholder, time or method for which the point weakens), not a late "on the other hand" paragraph. Cambridge's recurring complaint is balance that is brief, dismissed or unfocused.

**Band 4 floor.** A valid introduction, a real OV, a rebuttal with an example, at least one full SV with two examples, a qualifier and a terminal, and a second angle in SV2 or the conclusion. A missing SV2 lowers the ceiling; a missing rebuttal or a straw OV lowers the floor.

**If time runs out.** R keeps three sentences and its counter-example. SV1 keeps the full skeleton. SV2 shrinks to a topic sentence, both examples in one line and the *therefore*. The conclusion states the stand and one limit. Never write SV2 before SV1 is complete, and never pad R to fill space.

**Conclusion (three jobs).** Restate the stand, add value, and end on the moral of the story: a call to action, a reason for hope that has some basis, or precautionary advice.

## 4. Topic sentences

Most Band 3 essays fail at the topic sentence, before any example is written (Dr Chan, *Why Essays are in B3 Content*). The conditional form fixes most faults at once: "X is justified **when** …", "Y fails **where** …", "Z matters most **for** …".

| Fault | Why it fails | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Listing benefits instead of answering the keyword | "AI improves productivity" does not show that fear of AI is unjustified, because a society can gain from a technology and still have good reason to fear it. | Name the setting in which the keyword's claim stops holding: "Fear of AI is less justified in supervised research, where experts verify its output." |
| Treating a whole society as one group | "People can easily retrain" ignores differences in income, training and exposure. | Name the group for whom the claim holds: "workers whose roles AI complements and who can afford to retrain". |
| Confusing existence with justification | Widespread anxiety proves that people are afraid, not that their fear is well founded. | Replace popularity with a basis: consequential decisions, missing safeguards, no means of redress. |
| Treating every case as equally serious | "All AI is intrusive" ignores differences in purpose, data and safeguards. | Specify the conditions that make the claim strongest, and concede where it applies less. |

## 5. Examples

### 5.1 Two examples, then a judgement neither gives alone

Describe each example in one or two lines; the analysis should be longer than the description. Avoid "Example A shows X. Example B also shows X." Write "Taken together, A and B reveal that …", followed by one of these moves (Dr Chan, *Evaluating Two Examples in an SV Paragraph*; models use the CrowdStrike outage and the Change Healthcare cyberattack).

| Move | What the sentence does | Model |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship | States how one variable changes another | As more essential services share digital infrastructure, the failure of one part can affect many others. |
| Different causes, common issue | Shows that unlike causes produced like outcomes, so the cause lies deeper | One failure was accidental and the other deliberate, yet both caused major disruption, so the weakness lies in dependence on shared systems rather than in either threat. |
| Trend over time | Places the cases in a shift from past to present | Vulnerability has moved from isolated points of failure to systemic risks that spread across organisations and borders. |
| Transfer | Applies the insight to another sector or place | The same weakness applies to energy, transport and finance wherever they depend on common providers. |
| Who bears it | Shows who suffers the consequence | People suffer disruption even when their own devices did not fail, because the institutions they rely on are connected to the system that did. |
| Contrasting dimensions | Shows each case revealing a different side of one issue | One case shows how far disruption spreads; the other shows how deeply it harms an essential service. |
| Benefit and risk together | Shows that the advantage and the danger have one source | The interconnection that makes services efficient also creates the pathways through which failure spreads. |

With only one example, choose one lens (scale, speed, reach across sectors, cascading effects, concentration of risk, loss of control, difficulty of containment) and draw the larger judgement from it (*Strengthening EV and CU with One Example*).

### 5.2 Argument adaptation

One argument built on one well-understood example can answer several questions, provided the topic sentence is re-aimed at each question's keyword and the explanation draws out the feature of the example that the new question cares about (Dr Chan, *Content Update: Mass Media, Politics and Governance*). The Panama Papers support "the media can be trusted" through journalists' verification, and "the media supports democracy" through the accountability that verification made possible. The anchor bank (`02`) is built on this method. Re-aim the example; never retell it.

### 5.3 Singapore examples

A Singapore example must show a feature of Singapore (`09` §A), not decorate the paragraph with a scheme name. Pair a success with the group it misses, or the paragraph reads as government publicity: Circuit Breaker showed a state able to shut down a city, and the dormitory outbreaks showed whose housing the economy had accepted not looking at.

### 5.4 Foreign examples in "your society" titles

The 8881 syllabus rewards understanding of issues at local, regional and global levels and the ability to adapt ideas across time and place, so a foreign example can earn credit in a "your society" essay. It earns credit only when it does work that Singapore evidence cannot do alone.

Four uses work. A **contrast** shows what Singapore's design prevents or causes: Nordic family spending far above Singapore's still has not restored births, which shows that the problem lies beyond what money can buy. A **pressure arriving from abroad** shows a global force reaching Singapore: the Hormuz closure raised Singapore's electricity and food prices. A **forecast** shows where Singapore's trend leads: South Korea's fertility rate of about 0.72 shows the path below Singapore's 0.87. A **reform test** shows what happened when another society tried the policy under debate: Korea's removal of "killer questions" in 2023 was followed by record spending on private tuition.

Two uses fail. A false equivalence treats the foreign case as if it described Singapore ("American schools are failing, so Singapore's are too"). Padding retells a foreign case because it is memorised.

Keep the Singapore material in charge. In a P1 "your society" essay, open and close every paragraph on Singapore; a pressure from abroad gets one sentence, and a contrast gets two or three because it must name the feature Singapore has and the other society lacks, in no more than one paragraph in three. In an AQ, the passage usually describes another society already, so the comparison is built in; a further foreign case gets at most a clause ("unlike countries where tuition is a luxury, in Singapore it is close to universal"). The test: if the sentence after the foreign example can begin "in Singapore, by contrast" or "this reaches Singapore through", the example is working. If the honest next sentence is "similarly, in Singapore", cut the foreign example and spend the space on a second Singapore one.

### 5.5 Parallels from another topic

A title such as "To what extent should the beauty industry be regulated?" (2022 TJC) can borrow a parallel from food or tobacco regulation, on three conditions. The essay must name the shared reason for regulating: in both cases sellers know more about the product's risks than buyers do, so the state can justify correcting what consumers see. The parallel must add something the beauty evidence cannot: Nutri-Grade shows that a state can change behaviour with labels instead of bans. And the essay must say where the parallel breaks, because that break is the qualifier: sugar's harm shows up in a hospital bill paid partly by the public, while much of the harm from beauty marketing is to self-image, which is harder to measure and easier to call a private choice.

The main evidence must still come from the title's own domain. For beauty, `02` A14 gives CASE's 2025 complaint figures and the retouched-photo labelling laws in Norway and France, and `09` §K adds the protections that already exist. Order the paragraph beauty example first, borrowed example second, then the shared reason, then the limit.

Tobacco is a weak parallel for beauty: no amount of tobacco is safe, nicotine is addictive and second-hand smoke harms bystanders, while most cosmetics are safe and chosen as self-expression. Borrowing tobacco's justification invites the rebuttal "so should lipstick be banned?". Tobacco works only as the extreme case that shows where bans are justified, such as cosmetics containing mercury, which the Minamata Convention phased out from 2020.

A parallel supports an argument; it does not prove it. "The state regulates sugar, so it should regulate beauty" follows only if the shared reason has been shown. Borrow across topics when the title is really about a principle (regulation, freedom, state intervention) and the named area is one instance of it. Stay inside the area when the title tests knowledge of the area itself, as most beauty, sport and arts titles do. Keep at least one in-domain example in every paragraph, and borrow at most one sentence per paragraph, not in every paragraph.

### 5.6 Range across regions

Dr Chan recommends preparing examples from the West, East Asia, the Nordic states, isolated authoritarian states (North Korea, Myanmar, Cuba) and Singapore. Treat this as a checklist for range, not a quota. The 8881 Band 5 descriptor rewards "a wide range of relevant illustration", and the Cambridge report criticised scripts whose examples came from one place or were simply listed.

| Title | What range means |
|---|---|
| Global titles ("the world", "today", "countries") | Use examples from at least three contexts across the essay, and let no OV or SV rest on US evidence alone: pair each US case with one from elsewhere. The rebuttal is exempt because it carries a single counter-example by design. |
| Your society | Build every paragraph on Singapore, with at most one foreign contrast in the essay (§5.4). |
| Absolute claims ("always", "never", "only") | An extreme case is the cleanest limit: North Korea and Cuba show what sanctions cannot achieve (`03` §J). |
| Arts, sport and value titles | Range comes from different stakeholders, not different countries. |

Where each region sits in the anchors is listed in `02` (*Range check*). Write "an isolated authoritarian state" rather than "a rogue state"; the second is a judgement the essay has not earned.

### 5.7 Precision

Use only figures you are sure of, and date the volatile ones ("in October 2026"). Do not invent reports, surveys or percentages. Markers notice these, and one false fact casts doubt on the rest. Do not claim that a policy succeeded or failed without the yardstick and the evidence: "No Child Left Behind ruined American education" is an unsupported causal claim, whereas "schools taught to the test, and under-funded schools were penalised for results they lacked the means to achieve" names a mechanism.

## 6. Evaluation and terminals

### 6.1 What insight, evaluation and conceptual understanding are

Markers do not want a separate insight paragraph. They want these moves inside each paragraph: frame vague words (*progress, enough, ruin*); show a mechanism, not a correlation; weigh which factor is decisive and why; state conditions and limits; compare across place, stakeholder or time; draw an implication rather than a summary; test a counterfactual ("if X were absent, would the claim still hold?").

The syllabus wording to echo, not paste: trends, relationships and connections across issues; adapting ideas across time and place; local, regional and global significance; implications for individuals and society.

Use one evaluative lens visibly per paragraph: ideal against reality, short run against long run, majority against minority, theory against practice, or a split between stakeholders. Use one strong modal per claim (*can, should, must, will not*), never a stack such as "might perhaps could".

### 6.2 Ten principles to extract after two examples

1. Most hard cases are trade-offs between two goods, not stories with villains.
2. A crisis usually exposes weaknesses that already existed; it rarely creates them.
3. Costs fall unequally, so name who pays and who is spared.
4. Public trust is often the scarcest resource, and policies fail without it.
5. The design of a system (its rules and incentives) explains outcomes better than the character of the people in it.
6. A society can mobilise for a short emergency without being able to sustain the effort for years.
7. Distinguish benefits that happened by accident from outcomes a policy was designed to produce.
8. Small states are shaped by decisions of larger powers that they cannot vote on.
9. When every player is trapped in a race, no single player can stop first; only a rule that binds all of them at once ends it (`04` §H).
10. GP has no solutions: judge a policy against a stated standard of success.

### 6.3 The default terminal: Insight, Qualifier, Implication

> X drives Y **because** Z, **so long as** [condition]; **therefore** [implication for the title's keyword].

This sentence ends the paragraph. Asking "so what?" again after it starts either a new paragraph or a slippery slope.

**Stop when all three are true.** The last line contains the title's keyword (*lives, fault, doomed, value, enough*). It lands on a good the essay already treats as worth protecting: the ability to change one's mind, childhood, health, public order, shared facts, dignity, a functioning state. And the next step would be speculation rather than evidence.

**Two links and a landing.** The first link is the mechanism (how X works). The second is the near consequence that can already be observed. The landing states that this consequence is the harm or value the title asks about. "And then democracy dies" is an illegal fourth link; a larger scale needs a new paragraph with its own two links. A slope treats what is possible as certain or skips the *because*: "this will inevitably lead to" is weak; "where this design exists, we already see" is strong.

**Write the last sentence first.** It must contain the title's keyword; then write only the links that make it true.

| Keyword | What a legal ending lands on |
|---|---|
| *our lives* | Autonomy, attention, relationships, childhood, work or health has already been reduced. |
| *fault* | A named institution had a lever and used it, or failed to. |
| *doomed* | The trajectory is or is not reversible, stated without overshooting. |
| *progress* | Who can live better, and on what timescale. |
| *necessary* | A goal that cannot be reached by the alternative. |
| *enough, effective* | The rung of the yardstick that was reached, and the one that was not. |
| *rather than* | Whether A acts independently of B or only through B. |

**Worked contrast.** A slope: "The algorithm gives dopamine hits, which leads to indoctrination, which leads to clashes, which lets elites capture the masses." Each "leads to" is asserted, not shown. A legal ending: "Social-media feeds show users more of what they already agree with, and we can already observe people living in one-sided information worlds, so they lose the everyday habit of changing their minds, which is part of directing one's own life."

### 6.4 Seven other endings

The template becomes a weakness when the marker can hear "so long as… therefore…" in every paragraph. Any ending that names the title's good or harm, states the condition under which the claim holds, and stops before a further prediction is legal. Use one construction per paragraph; stacking two restarts the "so what?" chain.

1. **Identity.** The consequence already is what the title asks about, so no "therefore" is needed. Best for *value*, *ruining our lives* and *what is the point of X*.
2. **Trade-off.** Name the two goods and say which was given up, and by whom. Best for *to what extent*, because the limit becomes the ending rather than a clause added on.
3. **Reversal.** Keep the other side's fact and turn its conclusion round: a state that planned for the ageing crisis years ahead and still left care missing made a choice, which is a harder charge than an accident. Best for *crisis*, *inevitable* and *fault*.
4. **Yardstick.** End on the rung that was reached and the one that was not. Best for *enough* and *effective*.
5. **Stakeholder.** Put the cost in one group's hands: the working adult pays twice, into a retirement account and for a parent's care. Best for *fair*, *equal* and *for whom*.
6. **Comparison.** Show that the obvious alternative fails the same test. Best after a paragraph about a policy.
7. **Scope.** Show that the example is the normal design rather than a scandal: CoreWeave is what the AI build-out looks like once the buildings are bought with debt. Best for *today* and *increasingly*.

Under time pressure the template is safer, because it forces the condition onto the page.

### 6.5 Value titles and fake endings

Some titles ask what something is worth rather than what happened. End these on a named human good, not a prediction: human experience (a life not reduced to work and consumption), a shared public world (something in common besides the job), memory (a society that is more than an economic machine), civic speech (a language for disagreement), and recognition (seeing someone beyond their role). For "nothing more than a luxury" titles, the OV shows how a society ranks the thing under strain, and the SVs show what the good still is, because a low ranking is not the whole truth of "nothing more than".

Fake endings answer no title: economic salvation, soft power as foreign policy, the collapse of civilisation, World War Three, "dictatorship and then the end of the world", and "China is authoritarian".

**If time runs out:** "This matters for [keyword] because it already undermines [one basic good], not because of a hypothetical later collapse."

## 7. The planning sheet

The sheet is for R and the SVs only. Fill the *therefore* line of every block first, then find the examples that earn it; the recurring fault in the Q1, Q2 and prelim plans was architecture and examples arriving before terminals.

```text
R:    The OV overstates because ___
      Counter-EG: ___ (one named case that breaks the OV's logic)
      Attack line: the OV explains [X], not [all of X]

SV#:  Noun: ___ (one word, different for each SV)
      Therefore: ___ [title keyword]          <- write this first
      TS: [keyword] ___ when / where ___
      EG1: ___ (who, what, when)
      EG2: ___ (shares a frame with EG1)
      Taken together: ___ (one move from 5.1)
      Because: ___
      So long as / for whom: ___
```

**Pre-flight (thirty seconds).** R has a named counter-example that attacks the OV's own logic, not a different country or group. SV1 and SV2 have different nouns. Each SV has two examples and a *therefore*. No example appears in both R and an SV. R is shorter than one SV. Every topic sentence and last line contains the title's keyword. Every claim of policy success has a yardstick.

## 8. Openers and combinations

Fill the blanks; use one per essay at most.

1. Crisis exposes: "[Event] did not create [problem]; it removed the option of ignoring it."
2. Unequal cost: "Everyone faced the same shock; not everyone paid the same cost. [A] was inconvenienced; [B] faced catastrophe."
3. Trade-off: "This was not villainy but a choice between [good 1] and [good 2], and each side then treated its choice as proof of its own virtue."
4. Design: "[System] was built to maximise [measure]; [measure] is not the public good the title is asking about."
5. After-effects: "The emergency ended. The habits it created, such as distrust of authority, dependence on screens and anger over prices, did not."
6. Small state: "[Large power] decided [X]. Singapore had no vote in that decision, but it paid the price."
7. The race: "Every [player] could see the race was wasteful; none could afford to stop first."

**Combining two cases for a very broad title.** Use one case to explain where a pressure came from and the second to show what people did with it. COVID taught many people to treat official statements as one faction's opinion; by 2024 that attitude shaped the American election. Do not retell 2020 and then also retell the election.

## 9. What markers reward and punish

The official Cambridge reports for 8881 are circulated inside schools and are not public; the points below come from public sources. If the school releases the 2024 or 2025 reports, check this section against them.

**The band descriptors (8881).** Band 5 needs "fully appropriate and wide-ranging illustration … developed throughout", and reaches 28–30 only if "examples are consistently evaluated". Band 3 illustration is "narrow in range and/or underdeveloped". Conceptual understanding includes applying ideas to "changes in time and/or place". On "your society" titles, contrasts with other societies "may also be relevant … as long as the primary focus remains rooted in the specified society".

**Cambridge (2023 report, 8807).** The strongest essays used examples "to illustrate contrasts, trends, exceptions, and corroboration". Weaker essays listed or described examples, name-dropped, drifted into retelling, or cited "(possibly fictitious) reports … and statistics". On a "your society" title, weaker essays listed Singapore's schemes and "tended to assume their positive impacts and success"; stronger ones examined "the gap between theory and reality" and the groups left out.

**School reports.** ACJC (2024 prelim, 8881): weaker essays listed examples instead of evaluating reasons, raised counter-arguments only to dismiss them at once, treated "I agree to a large extent" as if it were nuance, and used flowery language. ASRJC (2019): weaker essays "contained examples only from one society, despite this not being a question requirement", and cited "a study showed" without saying who, where or when. VJC (2024, AQ): labels such as "fast-paced" and "meritocratic" earned credit only when explained for the specific point (`09` §A2).

**Recurring faults in your own plans, and their fixes.**

| Fault | Where it appeared | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The rebuttal attacks a different case instead of the OV's logic | Prelim migrants essay | Write the counter-example on the sheet before the rebuttal's stand, and test it against the OV's own absolute word. |
| SV1 continues the rebuttal's noun | Prelim migrants essay | Apply the chain test; give SV1 a noun the rebuttal does not touch. |
| A keyword is left unanswered (*welcome* treated as *admit*) | Prelim migrants essay | Put the keyword in every last line. |
| No qualifier on the page | Prelim migrants and Q1 plans | Write the *so long as* line on the sheet before any example. |
| Examples and structure before terminals | Q1 and Q2 plans | Fill the *therefore* lines first. |
| Planning overran (about 30 minutes for Q1) | Q1 plan | Use the selection rule; plan only R and the SVs. |
| A dated or vague example ("2000s Middle East") | Prelim migrants essay | Use the anchor's dated facts; name the year, actor and place. |
| Every example from the US in a global title | Q1 democracy plan | Run the range check in §5.6 before writing. |
| A policy's success is assumed (art lessons, museums) | Q2 arts plan | State the yardstick and name the group the policy misses. *Charlie Chan Hock Chye* is strongest used as a gap between official disapproval and recognition abroad (`06` §D). |
| An overclaimed cause ("No Child Left Behind ruined the education system") | Education draft | Name the mechanism and limit the claim (§5.7). |
| Degree statements ("not true to a great extent") and flowery phrasing ("under the shadows", "clinically efficient society") | Arts and politics drafts | Use the conditional stand; replace each metaphor with the literal claim (writing rule in `00`). |

## 10. The AQ in brief

There is no introduction. Name Singapore in the first line and give a nuanced stand: a real concern and a judgement on it. The OV and the rebuttal each need at least one example. An SV uses two local examples layered so that both reveal the same underlying issue. A one-word label (`09` §A2) may be stated without support only when it is tied to the specific point and taken down to its root at least once per answer. A foreign case gets at most one clause (§5.4). The last line of each paragraph answers the AQ prompt, not "Singapore has schemes".
