# Audit essay: corporations and governments

**Title:** *'Corporations, rather than governments, shape the world.' To what extent do you agree with this view?*

**Purpose.** A test of the restructured arsenal (October 2026): every step follows the workflow in `essay arsenal/00`, every fact comes from the arsenal, and every point where the arsenal helped or got in the way is recorded. The essay is a model of the method, not a script to memorise.

---

## Step 1. Select (three minutes)

**Keyword trap.** *Rather than* makes the title a comparison with an exclusive claim: corporations instead of governments. The keyword table in `01` §6.3 says a *rather than* ending must land on whether A acts independently of B or only through B. That decides the line of argument before any example is chosen: the strongest disagreement is not that governments are more powerful, but that corporations usually act *through* governments.

**Category.** The compilation files this kind of title under politics or globalisation, and the chooser in `02` had no entry for business titles. The cross-category line added during this audit now routes corporations titles to A5, A6 and A4, with depth in `03` §H, `04` §H and `06` §C.

**Anchors whose "proves" line answers the keyword.**

| Block | Example | Source | Why it fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| OV | The AI investment race among American technology firms | A5 (facts 2 and 3), `04` §H | Executives' own words show their spending answers to rivals, not to any government. |
| OV | South Korea's chaebol | `03` §H | Politicians need the conglomerates as much as the conglomerates need them. |
| R | The Starshield slowdown in Ukraine | `03` §G, `06` §C | The firm owned the infrastructure, but the decision on how to use it was reportedly political. |
| SV1 | The Iran war, the Fed's rate rise and CoreWeave | A4, A5 (fact 1) | A government's war and a central bank's rate rise changed the fortunes of the most celebrated industry. |
| SV1 | Beijing's anti-involution campaign and the carmakers' pledge | A6 (facts 2 and 3) | A government ordered an industry to change its behaviour. |
| SV2 | The global minimum tax and the G7 exemption | A7 (new "same pattern" line), `04` §H | A superpower shielded its own firms from other states' rules. |
| SV2 | Singapore's data-centre allocation | `06` §C | A small state set conditions for the world's largest data-centre operators. |

**Friction found.** Three of the seven examples (the chaebol, the global minimum tax, the data-centre allocation) sat only in the pillars, outside the memorised load. The minimum tax recurs in three pillars, so it has been attached to A7. The other two stay as lookup depth: they are used only when the title is about business and the state.

## Step 2. Unpack (two minutes)

- **Stakeholders.** Multinational corporations (technology firms, carmakers, conglomerates); governments of three kinds (superpowers, small states, local governments); and the people affected by both (workers, suppliers, citizens of states at war).
- **Aspects.** Economic (investment, prices, the cost of borrowing), political (regulation, war), technological (infrastructure ownership).
- **Levels.** Global (tax rules, wars), national (Korea, China, Singapore), local (Chinese provinces).
- **Time.** The question is about the present, but the claim rests on a trend: firms now own infrastructure that states once built themselves.
- **Angle.** Comparative extent. Compare like with like on the same measure, and test whether corporate power operates independently of governments or through them.
- **Defining "shape the world".** To decide what is built, what people pay and what they are permitted to do. Without this definition, the essay slides into listing which actor is bigger.

---

## Step 3. Stand

> Corporations shape the pace and products of the world economy, but governments still set its terms. Corporations take the lead only where governments compete with one another for their investment, and even then they usually act through a government rather than instead of one.

This names conditions (where governments compete; through, not instead of) rather than a degree ("to a large extent").

## Step 4. Planning sheet (*therefore* lines first)

```text
R:    The OV overstates because it treats owning infrastructure as controlling it
      Counter-EG: Starshield throttled in October 2026 while US officials pressed Kyiv
      Attack line: the OV explains who owns the network, not who decides its use

SV1:  Noun: conditions
      Therefore: governments decide the direction; corporations decide the speed
      TS: governments shape the world more wherever they set the conditions every firm depends on
      EG1: Iran war, oil near $120, Fed raises rates, CoreWeave priced to default (2026)
      EG2: 17 carmakers' 60-day pledge (June 2025); Xi's order (July 2025)
      Taken together: different causes, common issue (accident and command)
      Because: every firm's plans rest on the cost of credit and the right to sell
      So long as: the decision binds all firms at once (China's local governments do not)

SV2:  Noun: rivalry between states
      Therefore: corporate power is a product of state rivalry, not a replacement for state authority
      TS: corporations come closest to shaping the world where governments compete for them
      EG1: 2021 global minimum tax; 2025 G7 exemption for American-owned firms
      EG2: Singapore's 200 MW allocation with conditions (August 2026)
      Taken together: contrasting dimensions (a superpower shields; a small state disciplines)
      For whom: a small state with no scarce asset must bid with incentives
```

**Pre-flight.** R has one named counter-example that attacks the OV's logic (ownership is not control) rather than a different case. SV1 and SV2 have different nouns. Each SV has two examples and a *therefore*. No example is reused. Every topic sentence and last line contains *corporations* and *governments*. Range: the US, South Korea, Ukraine, the Middle East, China, the G7 and Singapore, with no OV or SV resting on US evidence alone.

**Endings chosen** (`01` §6.4) so the marker does not hear the same template five times: the rebuttal uses a reversal, SV1 the default template, SV2 the *rather than* landing, and the conclusion precautionary advice.

## Step 5. The essay (about 890 words)

**Introduction.** Private firms now own much of the infrastructure the world runs on, from data centres to satellite networks. It is tempting to conclude that corporations, rather than governments, decide how the world develops. Yet the same years have seen governments start wars, raise the price of borrowing and order whole industries to change course. Platforms plainly shape culture, deciding much of what people watch and read; but if shaping the world means deciding what is built, what people pay and what they are permitted to do, then corporations shape the pace and products of the world economy, while governments still set its terms. Corporations take the lead only where governments compete with one another for their investment, and even then they usually act through a government rather than instead of one.

**Opposing view.** Those who agree point to who now builds the world's infrastructure. Artificial intelligence is being built by a handful of American firms whose spending answers to their rivals rather than to any government: Alphabet's chief executive said in 2024 that the risk of underinvesting was "dramatically greater" than that of overinvesting, and Mark Zuckerberg accepted in 2025 the risk of "misspending a couple of hundred billion dollars". Their orders help explain why TSMC reported record profits in October 2026, so a contest among a few firms now decides what the world's most advanced factories make. In South Korea, Samsung, SK, Hyundai and LG account for so large a share of output and employment that politicians need them as much as they need politicians. Where firms own what an economy runs on, governments follow corporations' investment decisions rather than direct them.

**Rebuttal.** This view is right that firms own the infrastructure, but it assumes that ownership is the same as control. The clearest test came in October 2026, when the military satellite network on which Ukraine's drones depend, owned by a single American company, was throttled for more than a day while US officials pressed Kyiv to stop striking Russian refineries. Two officials told Reuters that the order came from the White House, which denies it. If their account is right, the network was private but the decision about how to use it in a war was political, so at its most decisive moment the company's power served a state's diplomacy instead of rivalling it.

**SV1 (conditions).** Governments shape the world more than corporations wherever they set the conditions on which every firm depends: the price of borrowing and the permission to compete. When the US and Israel struck Iran in February 2026 and Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz, oil rose towards $120 a barrel and the Federal Reserve raised interest rates; by October, markets priced CoreWeave, which owes about $50 billion, as more likely than not to default. In China, seventeen carmakers pledged under regulatory pressure in June 2025 to pay suppliers within sixty days, and in July Xi Jinping ordered action against "disorderly low-price competition". Taken together, one government changed an industry's fortunes by accident, through war and interest rates, and the other by command, yet in both cases powerful firms bent to decisions they did not make. This is because every firm's plans rest on the cost of credit and the right to sell, which only states control. The claim holds so long as a government's decision binds all firms at once: China's local governments keep subsidising the industries Beijing condemns, so a state whose own officials are players in the race cannot end it alone. Governments therefore decide the direction in which the world economy moves, while corporations decide only how fast it travels within those limits.

**SV2 (rivalry between states).** Corporations come closest to shaping the world when governments compete for their investment, yet even this power runs through governments. In 2021 more than 130 countries agreed a 15% global minimum tax to end a decades-long race to cut corporate tax; in June 2025 the G7 exempted American-owned companies from key parts of it, after Washington dropped a proposed retaliatory tax on foreign firms. Singapore, with far less leverage, still decides which data centres it hosts: in August 2026 it allocated 200 megawatts among four operators, chosen from more than twenty bids, on condition that they draw at least half their power from lower-carbon sources. Taken together, the two cases show corporate influence bounded by whichever government controls what a firm needs: a superpower shields its firms from other states' rules, and a small state disciplines the firms it courts by rationing its scarce electricity. A small state with no such asset must bid with tax incentives instead, and there the firms hold the upper hand. Corporate power is therefore greatest where governments refuse to bind themselves together, which makes it a product of their rivalry rather than a replacement for their authority.

**Conclusion.** Corporations shape what the world builds and buys, but governments still set the terms on which they do so, and the firms that look most powerful often act through a government rather than against one. The greater danger is therefore not that corporations will replace governments, but that governments, racing one another for investment, will stop setting terms at all. The Montreal Protocol shows that rival states can bind themselves together when a rule covers every major producer; the weakening of the global minimum tax shows how quickly that protection fades once the largest player opts out.

## What the audit changed in the arsenal

1. **The chooser had no route for business titles.** `02` now has a line for titles that cut across categories.
2. **The range rule was unworkable for this title.** "No more than one US-led paragraph" fails when the world's largest firms are American. `01` §5.6 now requires each OV and SV to pair a US case with one from elsewhere, and exempts the rebuttal, which carries one example by design.
3. **A recurring fact sat outside the memorised load.** The global minimum tax is now one sentence under A7.
4. **A chronology trap appeared while drafting.** The carmakers' pledge (June 2025) came before Xi's order (July 2025), not after it. The anchors give months, which is what caught it; keep months on every volatile fact.

## Shortcomings of the style and strategy, and how to fix them

The evidence for each point comes from the post-mortems of the Q1, Q2 and prelim plans, the faults table in `01` §9, the stress test in `essay planning/03`, and this audit. Each fix keeps the two features worth protecting: a small memorised load, and pillars deep enough to supply a second fact.

**1. The anchors pull essays toward the same cases.** Sixteen anchors make memorisation manageable, but they also make it tempting to force an anchor onto a title it does not fit, and Cambridge's 2023 report names "drifting into retelling" as a mark of weaker scripts. *Fix:* keep the order in the planning sheet. Write the *therefore* line before choosing the example, and if no anchor's "proves" line answers that sentence, use the value endings in `01` §6.5 and treat the anchor as one sentence of evidence at most. In the exam, a title the stress test marks Thin should usually be skipped.

**2. The newest anchors rest on figures that will move.** A2, A4 and A5 depend on events from 2025–26 whose numbers (oil prices, default-risk prices, the Gaza recovery plan) may change before the exam, and one wrong figure makes a marker doubt the rest. *Fix:* recheck those three anchors in the week before the paper, round figures you are unsure of ("nearly $120", "about $50 billion"), and remember that the mechanism earns the marks, not the decimal place. Each of these anchors should also have an older backup case in its pillar.

**3. Fact sentences get crowded.** Length itself is not the problem: Dr Chan's model essays run up to about 1,100 words, and the shortest, at just over 900, barely have room for examples. The problem is where the words go. Your natural sentence carries an actor, a mechanism and a qualifier at once, and when a fact sentence also carries three or four figures, it uses up the space and the writing time that the comparison and qualifier need. Under time pressure, crowded sentences are also where grammar breaks. *Fix:* keep each fact sentence to one event with at most two figures, let the analysis carry the length (about 200 words per SV), and practise writing the seven-sentence SV skeleton in twelve minutes by hand.

**4. The endings can become a template.** Insight, Qualifier, Implication is safe, but a marker who hears "so long as… therefore…" in every paragraph reads a formula rather than thought. *Fix:* choose each paragraph's ending at the planning stage from `01` §6.4, picking by keyword (reversal for *inevitable* or *fault*, yardstick for *enough* or *effective*, the *rather than* landing for comparisons), and keep the template for the paragraph written under the most time pressure.

**5. Singapore evidence drifts toward praise of government.** The Q2 arts plan assumed policies worked, and Cambridge reports that weaker "your society" essays "tended to assume their positive impacts and success". Your Singapore material is strong on state capacity, which makes this the easiest fault to fall into. *Fix:* pair every Singapore success with the group it misses (`01` §5.3), and run SIRDEC (`09` §F) whenever the title says *effective*, *enough* or *successful*.

**6. Range still runs through the US.** Five anchors are American, Southeast Asia has only Myanmar, and Africa and Latin America have none. For "the world" titles the range check covers this, but a title on poverty, aid or development has thin support. *Fix:* do not add anchors. Use COVAX (A11) and the climate-finance gap (`05`) as the developing-world angle, and choose another question when a title needs specific knowledge of Africa or Latin America.

**7. The default cross-out needs a clear reopening test.** Technology and media titles (about 18% of prelim questions) are crossed out at the start by choice: most of the cohort writes them, and the generic answers they invite are where Band 3 essays cluster. The strategy's advantage comes from the depth of the anchors in the remaining categories, since markers grade each script against the descriptors rather than against the cohort. Its one risk is a paper where the best remaining title is weak for you. *Fix:* reopen a crossed-out title only when it passes the selection rule and its real question is who controls a technology or who pays for it, as this title's was; A5, A8, A9 and `06` §C cover those (`01` §1).

**8. Planning overruns.** The Q1 plan took about thirty minutes and the prelim essay did not follow the rehearsed clock. *Fix:* plan only R and the SVs on the sheet, write the introduction and OV straight, and stop planning at fifteen minutes even if one line is blank: a blank qualifier can be filled while writing, while lost writing time cannot be recovered.

**9. Compressed phrasing returns under pressure.** The coherence rule exists because your drafts turn causal chains into coined phrases and metaphors ("under the shadows", "clinically efficient society"). Timed writing brings the habit back. *Fix:* before writing each topic sentence, check it names who acted, what they did and who was affected. If it does not, it is a label, not a claim.

**10. The memorised load is close to its limit.** Sixteen anchors carry about fifty facts and forty figures, and similar numbers (3.7%, 33 months, 58%, 65.6%) are easy to swap under stress. *Fix:* learn figures only where the hook depends on them, drill recall from the hook in the order set out in `02`, and if a figure will not come, write the fact without it ("factory-gate prices had fallen for almost three years").
