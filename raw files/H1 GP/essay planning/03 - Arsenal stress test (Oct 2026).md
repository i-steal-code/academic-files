The essay arsenal was tested against 30 prelim questions drawn from `essay resources/2022-2026 prelim essay questions (recategorised).docx`, using Dr Chan's October 2026 notes as the standard for what a Band 4–5 answer needs.

---

## Method

**Population.** 716 questions in 14 categories.

**Allocation.** Each category received 30 × (its share of 716) places, rounded down, and the remaining places went to the categories with the largest remainders. Crime and Punishment (5 questions, 0.7%) rounds to zero.

**Selection.** Within each category, questions were taken at a fixed interval (category size ÷ places) from a random starting point. Random seed 20261024, so the draw can be repeated.

**Judging standard.** For each question: can an anchor supply a main example whose mechanism answers the keyword, written as a conditional topic sentence that avoids the four pitfalls in `05` §3a, with a Singapore fork where the title asks for one?

| Verdict | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Ready** | An anchor answers the keyword directly, and the adaptation table or crosswalk shows how |
| **Usable** | An anchor fits through argument adaptation, but the paragraph needs a second fact from a case file or a careful re-aim of the topic sentence |
| **Thin** | No anchor fits the keyword. The essay would rest on value reasoning, so treat the title as one to pass over in the three-minute selection unless the values argument is already clear |

## What the test found

1. **The old arsenal was built for politics and science, which make up only about a third of the questions.** Politics, international politics and science account for 31.2% of the population, and six of the nine strong verdicts fell in those categories. The seven gaps came from the arts (two), the environment (two), media, sport, and a science question about gender equality; the first four of those categories alone are 29.2% of the population.

2. **The case files held enough content but were organised by case, not by question.** Ten of the fourteen anchors come from material already in `01`, `02`, `06`, `07` and `08`. The fix was mostly to extract each example's mechanism and limit, then show which questions it answers, as Dr Chan's argument-adaptation notes do.

3. **Four new examples were strictly necessary, and each covers at least three categories.** The Montreal Protocol and the Paris Agreement cover environment, international organisations and cooperation. The 2023 Hollywood strikes cover the arts, work and control of technology. South Korea's deepfake crisis covers gender, technology and crime. Joseph Schooling covers sport, credentialism, second chances and the conduct of public figures.

4. **Value titles cannot be anchored, and should not be forced.** Fantasy fiction, travel, a single world language and technology as a cure for loneliness ask what something is worth to people. An anchor forced onto them reads as a pre-written essay. They stay thin by design, and in an exam the three-minute selection should pass over them unless the values argument comes easily.

5. **The old memorisation load was larger than it looked.** Five files of nine facts, one mechanism and three pairs each came to about 45 facts and 15 pairs before any Singapore material. The new load is fourteen anchors, each with three short parts.

## Still open

- **Thin titles.** If you want an anchor for meaning-of-art questions, the strongest candidate is one work that changed public debate in Singapore, so that it also serves the "your society" titles. That would be a fifteenth anchor; add it only if you find yourself choosing arts titles in timed practice.
- **Crime and punishment** had no place in the sample. A9 (Korea's deepfake law) and the MINDEF response in A16 cover proportionate punishment; deterrence and rehabilitation questions would need a Singapore case such as the Yellow Ribbon Project, which is not yet in the arsenal and would have to come from the infopacks.
- **The figures in A2, A4 and A5 date from October 2026** and will move. Quote them as "in October 2026".
