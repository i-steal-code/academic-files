## 1. Every question: five habits

1. **Name the topic and the relation before you look at the options.** Short, unconnected questions are your weakest format (your note, 9 Nov). Naming the relation first stops you starting cold on each one.
2. **Underline what is asked,** plus any NOT, INCORRECT, magnitude, change, or force on X by Y. Reading the stem wrong is your most frequent leak (20 times in P2 and P3 practice). Once you've chosen, reread the stem's last line and check that your option answers that exact question.
3. **Draw for every question on forces, fields or circuits:** a 10-second sketch in the margin. All 4 statics questions in Epoch 5 were wrong, and so was a run of 6 field and induction questions (E5-76 to 81). Both are what happens when you skip the diagram. When you did redraw the set-up, it helped every time.
4. **Look at how far apart the options are before you calculate** (section 2, last row). It tells you how precise you need to be.
5. **Don't change an answer on the second pass** unless you find an actual error. You overturned a right answer in the Ch 10 taper.

## 2. Question types and how to beat them

These are the shapes the A-level P1 keeps using, with the technique for each one.

| Type | What it looks like | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| **Units** (Q1–2, most years) | Base units of B or ε₀; which equation is homogeneous | Write each quantity's base units in a column and match powers. Use known equations to convert (F = BIL gives B in kg A⁻¹ s⁻²). Don't reason about what the units mean. |
| **Estimates** | Weight of a phone; area of a cell; KE of a sprinter | Anchors: phone 150 g, adult 60–70 kg, person 1.7 m, sprinter 10 m/s, room 3 m high. The options differ by powers of ten, so the power of ten decides it. |
| **Uncertainty and errors** | % uncertainty in a derived quantity; random or systematic | Percentages add for × and ÷, and a power multiplies its percentage. For error type, ask whether it shifts every reading the same way (systematic, affects accuracy) or scatters them (random, affects precision). |
| **Free-body diagrams and N3L pairs** | Which force pairs with W; resultant force at the top of a flight | Draw the body alone. An N3L pair involves the **same two bodies swapped** and the **same type of force**. The common trap is the force between the floor and the brick. |
| **Three-force equilibrium** (most years) | Hinged rod; hanging sign; vector triangle | The three forces meet at one point, and the triangle closes with arrows chasing head to tail. Rule out triangles whose arrows don't chase, then resolve along the unknown force. |
| **Moments and centre of gravity** | Uniform rod; L-shaped sheet; torque | Take moments about the unknown force so it drops out. Use the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the **line of action**, not to the point where the force is applied. |
| **Upthrust** (3 of 5 years) | Ball in water on a string; floating ice | Weight = upthrust + tension, with upthrust = ρ(fluid) × V(submerged) × g. Write that line first. |
| **Elastic collision** (4 of 5 years) | Find a final velocity; v–u graph | Two linear equations: momentum, plus relative speed of approach = relative speed of separation. Never use the KE equation. Pick a positive direction, and put a minus sign on anything moving the other way. |
| **Area and gradient of a graph** | Work from F–x; impulse from F–t; v–t from a–t | Area under F–x is work, under F–t is impulse, under v–t is displacement. For a graph built from another, find its value at 3 points (start, where the gradient changes sign, end) and pick the option that passes through all three. |
| **Ratios and proportion** (gravitation, I = nAvq, inverse square, escape speed) | "By what factor…"; "same density, twice the radius" | Write the formula, decide what stays the same and what changes (your own habit), then scale. Area goes as diameter². Don't plug in numbers. |
| **Statements 1, 2, 3** | "Which statements are correct?" | Judge first the statement that appears in the most options, and mark ✓ or ✗ on each. One verdict often rules out half the options. |
| **NOT or incorrect** | "Which is not an example of resonance?" | Circle NOT. Then test each option against the definition and choose the one that fails. (The loudspeaker-not-resonance question came up in both 2022 and 2025.) |
| **Definitions** | g, e.m.f., critical damping, mass defect | The wrong options are near-copies with **one qualifier missing**: per unit mass, positive test charge, shortest time *without oscillating*, nucleus vs its separate nucleons. Choose the one that has every qualifier. |
| **Graph options** | E_K–t in s.h.m.; I–V with a diode; P–t | Check the intercept, then whether the gradient grows or shrinks, then one value you're sure of. For s.h.m. energy against time, E_K is zero at the extremes and peaks twice per period. |
| **Circuit changes** (thermistor or LDR, 3 of 5 years) | Temperature rises: what happens to the ammeters? | Write the arrow chain, one link at a time: T↑ → R↓ → R_total↓ → I↑ → IR across the fixed resistor ↑ → p.d. across the thermistor ↓. In Epoch 3 you swapped current and p.d., so write V = IR at each link. |
| **Components in series, read off a graph** | Two I–V curves, 3.0 V supply | Same current through both. Find the current where the two p.d.s add up to the supply voltage. |
| **Potentiometer and balance point** | Which connection gives a balance? | At the junction, the two cells' positive terminals must face the same way. Rule out any option without a sliding contact. |
| **Field directions** (every year) | Electron deflected up and to the right: which E and B? Force on a wire beside a coil | Draw x, y and z axes in the margin. Work out **each deflection on its own** (up from one field, right from the other) and rule options out one direction at a time. For an electron, the conventional current runs **opposite** to its velocity. Use the right-hand grip rule for B from a current, then Fleming's left-hand rule for the force. |
| **Charged particle in E or B** | Proton between plates; r = mv/Bq; velocity selector | In E: constant force, so a parabola. In B: force ⊥ velocity, so a circle and constant speed. Ratio questions: r ∝ mv/q, and T = 2πm/Bq does not depend on speed. |
| **Multi-step numbers** (Q27–30: transmission loss, mass defect, energy released) | Options to 3 s.f. that are close together | Use the booklet constants. Bank the setup line, then calculate. If the options are close, this is a second-pass question. |
| **Choose the combination** | "Which pair of values gives…" | Test each option against the condition. RI's own solutions do it this way (2023 Q6, 2022 Q18). |
| **Option spacing** (any calculation) | — | If the options differ by powers of ten, work out the power of ten only, with g = 10 and π² ≈ 10. If they differ by factors of 2 or 4, check the step with the ½, the square or the root. If they're close, calculate properly on the second pass. If the unknown is inside an exponential or a trig function, test the middle option and narrow it down. |

## 4. When you're stuck

1. **Units, or a limiting case,** to cut the options down (m → 0, R → ∞, θ = 0° or 90°).
2. **Test a middle option** if the unknown is buried inside a function.
3. **Circle it and move on** after 90 s. Come back in the second pass.
4. **At 0:55, guess.** A blank always scores 0.
