ch 15 current of electricity

current as rate of flow of charge; I = nAvq idea
v = ds/dt, and n = number of charges per volume (m^3). ds * A gives change in volume, so nAv gives number of charges that flowed / dt which is rate of flow of charges. multiply by q again and we get rate of flow of charge, because flow of charge is number of charges flowing * charge of each charge. 

resistance definition; ohmic vs non-ohmic (use V/I at the point)
resistance is directly proportional to potential and inversely proportional to rate of flow of charge. 
ohmic vs non-ohmic resistance 

resistivity; how R changes with length and area
R directly proportional to length and inversely proportional to area

pd, emf, internal resistance: terminal V vs emf
terminal V and emf are difference because batteries have internal resistance. emf that drives 8v of emf may become 7.8v because battery internal resistance takes a piece of the p.d. potential difference is the difference in potential across 2 points of a circuit.

electrical power and energy: VI, I^2R, V^2/R; what each applies to
P = IV, can be rewritten by substituting V = IR. P = I^2R for losses in resistor, P = IV for driving power

rms for sinusoidal AC; average power in a resistor
rms base formula is to find the square root of the mean of the squares of the value (literally root mean square). on sinusoidal graphs, people have kindly done the approximation for us so we only need to know that rms on a full-wave sinusoidal graph is x_0/sqrt(2), on a half wave is x_0/2, on square wave is x_0. if wave is unconventional, just use the manual calculation for root mean square

voltmeter / ammeter placement; ideal assumptions
voltmeter has infinite resistance and is placed parallel to the points measuring p.d. so that it does not take a snipper of p.d. and mess up the circuit (because no current wants to route to a component with inifinite resistance). 
ammeter has 0 resistance and is placed in series to the point on the circuit to measure current so that it does not have any I^2R losses but can accurately monitor the current. much like a flow rate gauge on a water pipe.

potentiometer balance: what is equal; why galvanometer reads zero
test circuit emf = resistance component of the potentiometer p.d. and results in test circuit having 0 p.d. supplied across it (hence galvanometer is 0, and when I = 0, p.d. across resistors of test circuit is 0 )
